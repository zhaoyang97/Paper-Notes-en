# ADIEE: Automatic Dataset Creation and Scorer for Instruction-Guided Image Editing Evaluation

**Conference**: ICCV 2025  
**arXiv**: N/A  
**Code**: [GitHub](https://github.com/SherryXTChen/ADIEE.git)  
**Area**: Image Editing Evaluation / Vision-Language Models  
**Keywords**: image editing evaluation, automated scoring, VLM fine-tuning, reward model, instruction-guided editing

## TL;DR

This paper proposes ADIEE, an automated pipeline for constructing a training dataset of over 100,000 samples for image editing evaluation. It fine-tunes LLaVA-NeXT-8B as an editing quality scorer and surpasses open-source VLMs and Gemini-Pro 1.5 on multiple benchmarks. The resulting scorer can also serve as a reward model to improve editing model performance.

## Background & Motivation

Instruction-guided image editing has advanced rapidly, yet effective automated evaluation remains a key limitation. Traditional metrics (MSE, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) fail to capture semantic alignment, while CLIP-based metrics struggle with long-form text and compositional relations. Using VLMs as judges is a promising direction, but open-source models are poorly aligned with human judgments, and closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o) are opaque and costly. The most critical bottleneck is the **absence of publicly available training data** for fine-tuning open-source VLMs as editing scorers — existing resources consist only of small-scale evaluation benchmarks with heterogeneous evaluation protocols.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

ADIEE comprises three components: (1) training data generation via editing models — nine text-guided editing methods are applied to the MagicBrush dataset to produce diverse edited outputs with heuristic score assignments; (2) data generation from multi-turn editing sequences — multi-turn sequences from SEED-Data-Edit are used to construct samples with continuous scores; and (3) the scoring model architecture — LLaVA-NeXT-8B is extended with a `[SCORE]` token, and a MLP decoder maps its embedding to a continuous score. The trained scorer can further serve as a reward model to fine-tune editing models.

### Key Designs

1. **Automatic annotation via editing models**: Editing training datasets naturally provide positive samples (ground-truth edits, high scores) and negative samples (original input images treated as editing outputs, low scores). Nine editing methods generate intermediate-quality samples, and a combination of CLIP directional similarity, CLIP image similarity, and DINO similarity heuristics automatically assigns three-level scores of 0, 0.5, or 1. A fine-tuned Qwen2-VL is used to generate input/target captions aligned with editing instructions, addressing the misalignment between captions generated by off-the-shelf VLMs and the intended editing semantics.

2. **Data augmentation via multi-turn editing sequences**: Given a multi-turn editing sequence $S = [I_0, p_1, I_1, \ldots, p_l, I_l]$, any pair of frames at two positions forms a valid evaluation sample. Scores for intermediate frames are linearly interpolated as $f = (k - j_1) / (j_2 - j_1)$, yielding training data with a continuous score distribution rather than only the three discrete values 0/0.5/1.

3. **VLM scoring architecture and reward model**: A special `[SCORE]` token is added to the vocabulary of LLaVA-NeXT-8B, whose embedding is decoded into a continuous score via an MLP decoder. Two reward fine-tuning strategies are supported: (a) reward-conditioned training — the score is appended to the editing instruction and set to the maximum value during inference; (b) reward feedback learning — the term $(10 - \text{score})$ is incorporated as an additional loss to directly update the editing model's gradients.

### Loss & Training

The scoring model is trained for 30 epochs on 6×H100 GPUs with a batch size of 192, LoRA rank 8, and a learning rate of 2e-5. Reward fine-tuning of the MagicBrush model runs for 10,000 steps with a batch size of 384 and a learning rate of 5e-5. The dataset consists of 93,915 samples from editing models and 21,382 samples from multi-turn sequences.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

| Method | AURORA-Bench Correlation | GenAI-Bench Accuracy | AURORA-Bench Accuracy |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| CLIP-D | 0.3080 | — | — |
| Gemini-Pro 1.5 | 0.4038 | 55.87% | 50.76% |
| InternVL2-8B | 0.3342 | 52.43% | 45.07% |
| **ADIEE** | **0.4734** | **59.90%** | **55.51%** |

Compared to the previous SOTA: AURORA-Bench correlation +0.0696 (+17.24%), GenAI-Bench accuracy +4.03% (+7.21%), AURORA-Bench pairwise accuracy +4.75% (+9.35%).

### Ablation Study

- Reward fine-tuning of MagicBrush improves the ImagenHub average score from 5.90 to 6.43 (+8.98%).
- Multi-turn sequence data contributes a continuous score distribution, substantially improving scoring granularity.
- Editing prompt fine-tuning (VLM caption alignment) is critical to data quality.

### Key Findings

- Editing training datasets can be repurposed as evaluation training data at no additional annotation cost.
- Continuous score distributions (from multi-turn sequences) outperform discrete scores.
- Using the scorer as a reward model yields significant improvements, validating the closed-loop between evaluation and generation.

## Highlights & Insights

- The data construction strategy is elegant — it exploits the inherent structure of existing editing datasets to obtain evaluation training data for free.
- The continuous score assignment method for multi-turn sequences is both simple and effective.
- The closed loop of evaluation → reward model → improved editing model demonstrates strong practical value.
- The `[SCORE]` token design is clean and avoids the instability of parsing scores from natural language outputs.

## Limitations & Future Work

- Automatic score assignment relies on CLIP/DINO heuristics, which may introduce bias.
- Validation is limited to instruction-guided editing; text-guided editing evaluation (requiring prompt pairs) is not addressed.
- The LLaVA-NeXT-8B backbone incurs non-trivial inference overhead, making large-scale real-time evaluation impractical.
- Training data is primarily sourced from MagicBrush; generalization to other editing distributions remains to be verified.

## Related Work & Insights

- Evaluation benchmarks such as VIEScore, ImagenHub, and AURORA-Bench provide established evaluation standards.
- The reward model approach is analogous to RLHF but uses scalar scores rather than preference pairs.
- The automated data construction methodology is potentially extensible to evaluation in video editing, 3D editing, and related domains.
