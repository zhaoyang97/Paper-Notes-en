# Preventing Model Collapse Under Overparametrization: Optimal Mixing Ratios for Interpolation Learning and Ridge Regression

**Conference**: ICLR 2026  
**OpenReview**: [https://openreview.net/forum?id=9RdhTvYbX0](https://openreview.net/forum?id=9RdhTvYbX0)  
**Code**: To be confirmed  
**Area**: Learning Theory / Model Collapse / High-Dimensional Statistics  
**Keywords**: Model collapse, synthetic data, overparametrization, ridge regression, optimal mixing ratio

## TL;DR
This paper provides **exact generalization error formulas** for an iterative training scheme under overparametrized linear regression ($p/n \to \gamma > 1$), where fresh real labels and synthetic labels generated by the previous model are mixed by ratio $w$. It proves that the optimal real data proportion for minimum $\ell_2$-norm interpolators converges to the inverse golden ratio $\varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$, while for ridge regression, the optimal ratio is always $\ge 1/2$, theoretically characterizing when model collapse can be prevented and when it is inevitable.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Modern AI models are increasingly trained on their own synthetic data, triggering "model collapse"—the progressive degradation of predictive performance across generations. A widely adopted mitigation strategy is "mixing": blending a portion of original real data with synthetic data in each training round to ensure test error remains bounded over many iterations.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Existing rigorous analyses of model collapse (Shumailov, Dohmatob, Bertrand, Dey & Donoho, Gerstgrasser, etc.) are almost entirely restricted to **low-dimensional** settings or **Gaussian feature** assumptions. For example, in low-dimensional Gaussian estimation, it has been shown that repeated use of synthetic data causes the covariance matrix to collapse to zero almost surely and sample means to diverge. These results are disconnected from the "overparametrized" regime where modern machine learning operates (parameters far outnumbering samples, with generalization relying on implicit regularization).

**Key Challenge**: Overparametrization is the critical regime for understanding deep network generalization, yet there is no theoretical characterization of whether or how mixing real data prevents collapse in this regime. What is the optimal proportion of real data? Is this ratio a constant or does it vary with the setting? No rigorous answers have been provided.

**Goal**: To answer two fundamental questions in overparametrized linear regression: (1) Can mixing fresh real data with synthetic outputs mitigate model collapse in overparametrized problems? (2) What is the **optimal mixing ratio** that minimizes long-term prediction error?

**Key Insight**: The authors place the problem within the **proportional asymptotic** framework ($n, p \to \infty$ and $p/n \to \gamma > 1$), a random matrix theory tool that successfully explains phenomena like double descent. Under this framework, the risk for both minimum-norm interpolators and ridge regression can be precisely characterized by the Stieltjes transform, allowing the influence of the "mixing ratio" knob to be analyzed via closed-form functions.

**Core Idea**: Use random matrix theory to derive **closed-form formulas for asymptotic risk** in iterative mixing. By decomposing risk into a variance term (affected by mixing ratio $w$) and a bias term (unaffected by $w$), the calculation of the "optimal $w$" becomes a solvable optimization problem—yielding the unexpected result of the golden ratio.

## Method

As a purely theoretical work, this paper does not present an algorithmic pipeline. The core consists of an iterative data augmentation protocol, a set of exact risk formulas, and the characterization of optimal mixing ratios.

### Overall Architecture

Assume the true model is linear $y = X\beta + \varepsilon$, where $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ and $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p$. Starting from an initial estimate $\hat{\beta}_0$ fitted on original data $(y, X)$, each round $t \ge 1$ performs three steps: (1) Sample **fresh** real responses $y_t = X\beta + \varepsilon_t$; (2) Generate **synthetic** responses $\tilde{y}_t = X\hat{\beta}_{t-1} + \tilde{\varepsilon}_t$ using the previous model; (3) Linearly combine them $wy_t + (1-w)\tilde{y}_t$ with mixing ratio $w \in (0, 1)$ (weight of real data) to fit a new estimate $\hat{\beta}_t$. The ridge regression version is:

$$\hat{\beta}_{t,\lambda} = (X^\top X + n\lambda I)^{-1} X^\top \big(w y_t + (1-w) \tilde{y}_t\big),$$

while the interpolator version uses the pseudoinverse $(X^\top X)^\dagger$. Note that the design matrix $X$ is fixed per round while real labels are resampled—consistent with the "fresh data augmentation" framework by He et al., here extended to the overparametrized regime.

The analysis focuses on **out-of-sample risk** $R(\hat{\beta}; \beta) = \mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\beta} - \beta\|_\Sigma^2 \mid X]$, which follows a standard bias-variance decomposition $R = B(\hat{\beta}) + V(\hat{\beta})$. The study examines the limit of this risk as $t \to \infty$ and $n, p \to \infty$, and identifies the $w$ that minimizes it.

Characterizing risk requires two spectral components: the limiting spectral distribution $H$ of the feature covariance $\Sigma$, and the distribution $G$ of the signal $\beta$ energy projected onto the eigenbasis of $\Sigma$. Using the (companion) Stieltjes transform $m(z)$ uniquely determined by $m(z)^{-1} + z = \gamma \int \frac{x}{1 + m(z)x} dH$, all risk formulas can be expressed as integrals of $m$ over $H$ and $G$.

### Key Designs

**1. Golden Ratio Optimal Mixing: Interpolator risk perceives $w$ only through variance**

To address the unknown optimal $w$ in overparametrization, the authors first analyze the minimum $\ell_2$-norm interpolator. Theorem 3.1 gives the double limit risk:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{t \to \infty} R(\hat{\beta}_t; \beta) = \sigma^2 c(w) V + b_\star B, \qquad c(w) = \frac{w^2 + (1-w)^2}{w(2-w)},$$

where $V = \frac{m'(0)}{m(0)^2} - 1$, $B = \frac{m'(0)}{m(0)^2} \int \frac{x}{(1 + m(0)x)^2} dG$, and $b_\star = \lim \|\beta\|^2$. The key insight is: **the bias term $b_\star B$ depends on neither $t$ nor $w$**. The mixing ratio impacts only the variance through the function $c(w)$. The optimal $w$ is the minimum of $c(w)$, which occurs at $w_\star = \varphi^{-1} = \frac{2}{1 + \sqrt{5}} \approx 0.618$ (the inverse golden ratio). This holds for **any** $\Sigma$ satisfying the assumptions. For isotropic cases $\Sigma=I$, risk simplifies further to $\sigma^2 c(w) \frac{1}{\gamma-1} + b_\star (1 - \frac{1}{\gamma})$.

This result lifts the "golden ratio weight" phenomenon—previously only observed in low-dimensional OLS—to high-dimensional overparametrization with general covariate distributions. It also characterizes the collapse boundary: if $w=0$ (pure synthetic), then $R \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$, proving collapse is inevitable; but for $w > 1/3$, variance decreases monotonically with $t$, and since $\varphi^{-1} > 1/3$, the risk under optimal mixing decreases monotonically, **provably preventing collapse**.

**2. Dynamic Mixing: Convergence of adaptive weights to the golden ratio**

The authors prove that if an adaptive weight $w_t^\star$ is chosen to minimize risk in each generation, it follows the recurrence:

$$w_t^\star = \frac{1 + w_{t-1}^\star}{2 + w_{t-1}^\star}, \qquad w_0^\star = 1,$$

which monotonically decreases and converges to $w_\star = \varphi^{-1}$. This means that even with an adaptive strategy, the **long-term asymptotic risk is identical to using a fixed $\varphi^{-1}$**. The benefit of dynamic scheduling is only visible at $t=1$ and is much smaller than the gap between $w=1$ (pure real) and $w=\varphi^{-1}$.

**3. Log-Convex Risk in Ridge Regression and the "Always $\ge 1/2$" Rule**

In ridge regression with regularization $\lambda$, bias and variance both depend on $\lambda, t, w$. Theorem 3.2 (isotropic case) gives $R = \sigma^2 c(w) V_\lambda + b_\star B_\lambda$, where $V_\lambda$ and $B_\lambda$ involve $m_1 = m(-\frac{\lambda}{w})$ and $m_2 = m(-\frac{\lambda}{2-w})$. The authors prove the limit risk is a **log-convex** function of $w$, ensuring a unique minimum. Theorem 3.3 characterizes the minimizer $w_\star(\lambda)$: (i) $w_\star(\lambda) \in [0.5, 1]$, (ii) $w_\star \to \varphi^{-1}$ as $\lambda \downarrow 0$, and (iii) $w_\star \to 1$ as $\lambda \uparrow \infty$. The core takeaway: **the optimal mixing ratio is always at least 50%**, meaning real data must always be weighted more heavily than synthetic data.

**4. General Covariance and Structured Models: Spectral geometry determines weights**

Theorem 3.4 provides general anisotropic results for any spectral measures $H, G$. The risk remains bounded for any $w \in (0, 1), \lambda > 0$ as $t \to \infty$, proving the necessity of mixing. Analyzing specific models: (a) **Random effects models** where $G=H$, yielding $w_\star \in [0.5, 1]$; (b) **Spiked covariance models** $\Sigma = I + svv^\top$, where bias varies with spike intensity $s$ and alignment $\theta$. Both models confirm that **spectral geometry determines the specific value of the optimal weight**, but the "$w_\star \ge 1/2$" rule remains universal.

**5. Beyond the Fresh Augmentation Framework: When collapse is inevitable**

To reflect realistic conditions, Section 5 examines three variants where "mixing is not a silver bullet." First, **no fresh real data**: reusing original $(X, y)$ with synthetic labels makes the optimal weight $w_\star = 1$, meaning synthetic data offers no improvement. Second, **time-varying covariates with fresh labels**: optimal weights still exist but bias now depends on $w$. Third, **time-varying covariates with partial fresh labels**: Theorem E.2 proves **there exist $\gamma$ ranges where model collapse cannot be prevented**. This clarifies that mixing prevents collapse only when fresh real labels are continuously injected.

### A Complete Example

For an isotropic interpolator with $\gamma=2$: if $w=0$ (pure synthetic), $c(0) = \infty$ and risk diverges (model collapse). If $w=1$ (pure real), $c(1)=1$, but variance reduction from synthetic data is lost. At $w = \varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$, $c(w)$ is minimized. The bias term $b_\star(1-1/\gamma)$ remains constant, while variance is minimized, resulting in the lowest long-term risk. This minimum point is invariant to the specific covariance structure (e.g., Isotropic vs. AR(1)).

## Key Experimental Results

Experiments are **numerical simulations** to verify theoretical formulas. $X = Z\Sigma^{1/2}$ with $Z_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and empirical risk is averaged over 100 trials.

### Main Results

| Setting | Validation Target | Key Finding |
|------|----------|----------|
| Interpolator, $\Sigma=I$ and AR(1), $\gamma \in \{1.5, 2, 3\}$ | Risk vs. Weight $w$ | Minimum point is always at $w_\star = \varphi^{-1}$ |
| Interpolator, Risk vs. Rounds $t$ | Convergence | Empirical risks stabilize within a few rounds |

### Ablation Study

| Configuration | Phenomenon | Explanation |
|------|------|------|
| $w=1$ vs. $w=\varphi^{-1}$ vs. dynamic $w_t$ | Dynamic and $\varphi^{-1}$ curves overlap for $t \ge 2$ | Fixed golden ratio is nearly optimal; scheduling has minimal gain |
| No fresh real data (Eq 5.1), $n=500$, 50 rounds | Risk optimal at $w_\star=1$ | Theoretical verification that reusing old data makes synthetic data useless |

### Key Findings
- **Golden Ratio Robustness**: For interpolators, $w_\star = \varphi^{-1}$ is independent of covariance structure and $\gamma$, verified by simulations across isotropic and strongly correlated covariates.
- **Dynamic Scheduling Utility**: Contrary to intuition, while $w_t^\star$ decreases per generation to add more synthetic data, the long-term risk is virtually identical to a fixed $\varphi^{-1}$.
- **Mixing Boundaries**: Without continuous fresh real labels, the optimal strategy reverts to pure real training ($w=1$). In some partial fresh label settings, collapse is inevitable regardless of mixing.

## Highlights & Insights
- **Quantifying Intuition**: While the rule of thumb is "use more real data," this paper quantifies it as $w_\star \ge 1/2$ and exactly $0.618$ for interpolation, providing specific actionable values.
- **Bias-Variance Decoupling**: In the interpolation case, the bias term's independence from $w$ reduces the optimization to a simple univariate minimization of $c(w) = \frac{w^2 + (1-w)^2}{w(2-w)}$, which is why the golden ratio emerges so cleanly.
- **Transferable Spectral Geometry**: By using $H$ (covariance spectrum) and $G$ (signal-eigenbasis alignment), the framework provides a template applicable to other overparametrized risk analyses.

## Limitations & Future Work
- **Theoretical Constraints**: The analysis relies on random matrix theory and is currently limited to $\ell_2$ geometry. Implicit regularization limits for other $\ell_p$ geometries require tools beyond the current scope.
- **Idealized Model Settings**: The study uses linear models with proportional asymptotics and fixed/simple time-varying design matrices, which is a simplification of real-world generative model complexity (non-linearity, distribution shift, generator degradation).
- **Future Directions**: Qualitative conclusions could be extended to non-linear settings like Generalized Linear Models (GLM) using high-dimensional extensions.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. Shumailov / Dohmatob (Low-dimensional collapse)**: They proved covariance collapse and risk divergence in low-dimensional Gaussian settings; Ours extends the characterization to overparametrization and identifies the optimal mixing ratio to prevent it.
- **vs. Bertrand / Gerstgrasser / Dey & Donoho (Mixing Framework)**: They experimentally verified that mixing real and synthetic data bounds error in low dimensions; Ours provides exact risks and optimal $w$ in overparametrized regimes.
- **vs. He et al. (Golden Ratio OLS)**: The golden ratio was previously observed in low-dimensional OLS; Ours proves its validity for high-dimensional overparametrized interpolators across general covariate distributions.
