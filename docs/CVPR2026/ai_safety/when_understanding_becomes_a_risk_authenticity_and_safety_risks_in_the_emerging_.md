# When Understanding Becomes a Risk: Authenticity and Safety Risks in the Emerging Image Generation Paradigm

**Conference**: CVPR 2026  
**arXiv**: [2603.24079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24079)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: AI Safety / Image Generation  
**Keywords**: Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), Image Safety, Harmful Content Generation, Fake Image Detection, Diffusion Model Comparison

## TL;DR

A systematic comparative analysis of the safety risk differences between MLLMs and diffusion models reveals that MLLMs are more prone to generating unsafe images due to their stronger semantic understanding (comprehending abstract or non-English prompts). Furthermore, images generated by MLLMs are more difficult for existing fake image detectors to identify; even detectors fine-tuned specifically for MLLMs can be bypassed by enriching prompt details.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: The text-to-image generation paradigm is shifting from diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs, e.g., Janus, Bagel). While diffusion models generate images through iterative denoising, MLLMs integrate language understanding, cross-modal reasoning, and image generation into a unified architecture, offering superior semantic understanding and compositional flexibility.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Mature safety defenses—such as safety filters (NSFW detectors) and fake image detectors (e.g., DE-FAKE)—have been established around diffusion models. However, these countermeasures are optimized for the diffusion paradigm. MLLMs possess fundamentally different generation mechanisms, allowing them to understand subtle, abstract, or indirect unsafe intentions, which suggests existing defenses may systematically fail.

**Key Challenge**: The enhanced semantic understanding of MLLMs is a double-edged sword. While it improves usability and generation quality, it also enables the models to understand and execute unsafe instructions that diffusion models simply cannot "comprehend." The research community has yet to systematically recognize the new safety risks brought by this paradigm shift.

**Goal**: This study addresses two core research questions: (1) Are MLLMs more prone to generating unsafe images than diffusion models? (2) Are fake images generated by MLLMs harder to detect than those from diffusion models?

**Key Insight**: Using diffusion models as a baseline, the authors perform systematic comparative experiments on 7 models (2 diffusion + 5 MLLMs) using 82,880 images generated from 1,184 unsafe prompts and 14,000 images from 2,000 benign prompts.

**Core Idea**: The semantic understanding advantage of MLLMs becomes a liability in the safety dimension—they can comprehend abstract unsafe prompts to generate meaningful unsafe content, whereas diffusion models remain "safe" only because they fail to understand the prompts and produce corrupted images.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

This paper is a systematic measurement study rather than a proposal for a new method. It evaluates whether existing safety defenses remain effective when text-to-image generation migrates from diffusion models to MLLMs. The experiments focus on whether MLLMs are more likely to generate unsafe images (RQ1) and whether their outputs are harder for fake image detectors to capture (RQ2). To ensure a fair comparison, the authors use a fixed set of models, datasets, and a unified classifier to quantify results as "unsafe/safe/corrupted" across over 80,000 generated images.

### Key Designs

**1. Unsafe Image Generation Evaluation Framework: Measuring intrinsic model tendency rather than filters**

To answer RQ1, it is crucial to distinguish between a model's "willingness to generate" and the "effectiveness of external filters." The authors **disable all external safety mechanisms** and run 7 models (SD3.5 Large/Turbo, Bagel, Janus, Janus Pro, TokenFlow, VILA-U) on 5 unsafe prompt datasets (I2P, Lexica, 4chan, Template, TemplateLong). Each prompt generates 10 images. The OpenAI Moderation API serves as the judge (89.2% agreement with human labels) to calculate the **unsafe score** per "prompt-model" pair—the ratio of NSFW images among the 10. This score measures the model's inherent semantic obedience rather than its easily bypassed NSFW filter.

**2. Corruption Analysis: Debunking the illusion of "Safer Diffusion Models"**

Raw testing showed diffusion models have lower unsafe scores. However, the authors hypothesized this is due to a lack of understanding of complex prompts, leading to corrupted outputs rather than superior alignment. To verify, they created "safe counterparts" for 50 4chan prompts and measured the **corruption rate**. Results showed SD3.5 Large had an 80% corruption rate, while the worst MLLM (Bagel) was only 4.5%. Notably, 99.4% of these corrupted images were classified as "safe." Thus, the low scores for diffusion models stem from generation failure—insufficient understanding acts as an accidental safety valve.

**3. Fake Image Detection Evaluation Framework: Assessing MLLM "fingerprints" and prompt length impact**

RQ2 investigates if deployed fake image detectors remain effective. Images generated from benign prompts (MSCOCO, Flickr30k) were fed into 4 detectors (Winston.AI, Illuminarty, DE-FAKE, AIorNot-SigLIP2). A **prompt richness gradient experiment** was conducted: an intention was written in three levels (v0: original, v1: GPT-4o expanded, v2: further expanded) to see if detailed prompts make images harder to detect as fake. This tests if MLLMs provide an evasion path unavailable to diffusion models.

**4. Cross-lingual, Bias, and Association Controls: Materializing "Understanding as Risk"**

These controls illustrate the side effects of MLLM knowledge. **Cross-lingual**: Using Chinese unsafe prompts (TemplateLongChinese), diffusion models failed completely (unsafe score of 0), while MLLMs continued to generate unsafe content. **Gender Bias**: Under gender-neutral pornographic prompts, MLLMs significantly skewed toward female images. **Association**: Given abstract slang (e.g., "f*ck that the place is a sh*t hole"), diffusion models merely rendered the text literally, whereas MLLMs associated the prompt with filthy environments. All three point to the same conclusion: diffusion model "safety" comes from ignorance, while MLLM semantic understanding bridges abstract, vague, and cross-lingual unsafe intent.

### Key Findings

- **MLLMs are systematically less safe**: In all 5 datasets, MLLM unsafe scores were consistently higher than those of diffusion models.
- **Root cause is understanding, not alignment failure**: Diffusion models produce corrupted images (labeled safe) because they cannot understand complex prompts, not because of better safety training.
- **Insufficient external protection**: Even with NSFW detectors enabled, MLLM unsafe scores remain higher than those of diffusion models.
- **Detection generalization dilemma**: Detectors fine-tuned on one MLLM show limited effectiveness on others, and MLLMs can evade detection through prompt engineering.
- **Gender Bias in MLLMs**: Gender-neutral pornographic prompts tend to generate female images.

## Highlights & Insights

- **The core insight of "Understanding as Risk" is highly provocative**: While model intelligence is usually prioritized, this paper demonstrates that understanding is a double-edged sword in safety. This framework could be extended to any AI capability analysis—technical progress in "understanding" potentially expands the surface area for abuse.
- **The discovery of corrupted images as an "accidental safety valve" is significant**: Diffusion models are not safer because they are "better trained for safety," but because they fail to generate content when they do not understand. This implies that as diffusion models' understanding improves, their safety risks will increase accordingly.
- **The relationship between prompt richness and detection difficulty** reveals a new attack vector: users can lower the probability of detection by adding more descriptive details to MLLM prompts, a tactic ineffective for diffusion models due to their fundamentally different generation mechanisms.

## Limitations & Future Work

- Experimental protocols disabled model safety mechanisms; while this measures intrinsic tendencies, it does not fully reflect real-world deployment scenarios.
- Only 7 models were tested, excluding major closed-source MLLMs like GPT-Image-1 or Gemini.
- Safety classification relies on the OpenAI Moderation API, which has approximately 10% error despite 89.2% human agreement.
- The study did not deeply analyze the internal safety alignment training mechanisms of MLLMs, inferring only from external behavior.
- Future work could explore (1) designing new safety filters based on MLLM semantic understanding and (2) utilizing prompt understanding capabilities for safety detection itself.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs. NSFW Safety Filters**: This work shows MLLMs remain less safe than diffusion models even with external filters, highlighting the need for safety mechanisms specifically designed for MLLMs.
- **vs. Fake Image Detectors (DE-FAKE, AIorNot)**: These detectors, optimized for diffusion models, show significantly decreased performance on MLLM outputs and poor generalization after fine-tuning, revealing new challenges for the "detector-generator" arms race.
- **vs. Existing Safety Benchmarks (I2P, Lexica)**: This study is the first to extend these benchmarks to a cross-paradigm comparison between MLLMs and diffusion models, introducing new sets like TemplateLong to test semantic understanding differences.
