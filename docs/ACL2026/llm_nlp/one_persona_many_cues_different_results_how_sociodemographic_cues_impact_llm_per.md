# One Persona, Many Cues, Different Results: How Sociodemographic Cues Impact LLM Personalization

**Conference**: ACL 2026  
**arXiv**: [2601.18572](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18572)  
**Code**: [GitHub](https://github.com/frawee/persona_cues)  
**Area**: LLM Fairness / Personalization  
**Keywords**: Persona Prompting, Sociodemographic Cues, LLM Personalization Bias, Ecological Validity, Prompt Robustness

## TL;DR

This paper systematically compares the effects of 6 common persona prompting methods (two variants each for name, explicit mention, and dialogue history) across 7 LLMs and 4 tasks. The study find that while average responses are highly correlated across prompting methods, the differences between personas generated by different methods vary significantly. Overly explicit prompts lead to stronger personalization bias, suggesting that bias conclusions should not be drawn based on a single prompting method.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Sociodemographic personalization in LLMs is becoming increasingly common—adjusting responses based on gender, race, or age has been shown to improve helpfulness. Researchers study biases in this personalization through "personas" (synthetic user profiles).

**Limitations of Prior Work**: (1) Existing studies typically use only one prompting method to convey a persona, ignoring the sensitivity of LLMs to prompt variations; (2) The ecological validity of different prompting methods varies greatly—explicitly mentioning "You are talking to a woman" rarely occurs in real interactions; (3) It is unclear whether using different prompting methods leads to different conclusions about bias.

**Key Challenge**: The choice of prompting method may dictate research conclusions—if one prompting method shows bias and another does not, which is more credible?

**Goal**: To systematically evaluate the impact of prompting method choices on the discovery of personalization bias and provide methodological guidance for future research.

**Key Insight**: Design a flexible evaluation framework covering three major categories of prompting methods (Name, Explicit Mention, Dialogue History) with two variants each, compared across multiple models and tasks.

**Core Idea**: Prompting method is a hidden degree of freedom in personalization research. Although the average effects of different prompting methods are correlated, they differ significantly in terms of variance; high ecological validity implicit prompting methods should be prioritized.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

This paper does not train any models; it addresses a methodological question: Does the way researchers "insert" a persona into a prompt change the final conclusions about bias? To this end, the authors build a flexibly configurable evaluation matrix: 10 personas (3–4 values each for gender, race, and age) × 6 prompting methods × 7 LLMs × 4 evaluation tasks. The 6 prompting methods are ranked from high to low "ecological validity" (naturalness in real interactions): Dialogue History > Name > Explicit Mention. The analysis focuses on three dimensions: the consistency of average responses across prompting methods, the variability in "inter-persona differences," and the alignment of each method with real human dialogue history.

### Key Designs

**1. Systematic Design of Six Prompting Methods: Covering the Full Spectrum from Implicit to Explicit**

Existing bias studies often use a single prompting method to convey a persona, making it impossible to judge if conclusions are tied to that specific choice. This paper breaks prompting methods into three categories with two variants each—Name (in system prompt / in user prompt), Explicit Mention (in system prompt / in user prompt), and Dialogue History (from real human interaction / synthetically generated)—covering the full spectrum from most natural to most artificial. Their ecological validity differs vastly: names often appear in metadata, and dialogue history is always present in real conversations, whereas explicit mentions like "You are talking to a woman" almost never appear in reality. The intent of this design is straightforward: if a "bias" emerges only under explicit mentions but not under implicit methods, it is likely a methodological artifact rather than a real issue.

**2. Multi-Dimensional Consistency Analysis: Distinguishing "Average Consistency" from "Difference Pattern Consistency"**

Highly correlated average responses between two prompting methods do not mean they tell the same story about bias. The paper first uses Spearman correlation to measure the consistency of average responses. More crucially, it examines which prompting method produces the largest inter-persona differences. The results show that high average correlations can mask disagreements in difference patterns—two methods might agree perfectly that "GPT-4 is better than Claude," yet disagree entirely on "how much better it treats men than women." Furthermore, more explicit prompts lead to stronger personalization bias.

**3. Baseline Comparison with Real Human Dialogue History: Using Real Interactions as a Benchmark for Synthetic Prompts**

Ultimately, researchers care about the biases real users encounter. Comparing synthetic prompts is insufficient without a real-world reference. This paper introduces real human–LLM interaction data from Kearney et al. (2025) as a benchmark, aligning bias patterns from synthetic prompts with real data. This allows for a determination of which method most closely approximates real-world personalization effects, leading to the methodological recommendation that high ecological validity implicit prompts should be prioritized.

### Loss & Training

This work involves no training; all evaluations are performed during inference across 7 LLMs: GPT-4o/4.1, Gemma-3-27B, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Llama-3.1-70B, Mistral-Small, and DeepSeek-V3.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

- Spearman correlations of average responses across prompting methods are typically $> 0.8$ (high consistency).
- However, inter-persona differences (bias magnitude) caused by explicit mentions are 2–3 times larger than those caused by implicit methods.
- Different prompting methods disagree on which persona combination produces the maximum difference.

### Key Findings

- Explicit (but unnatural) prompting methods lead to stronger personalization bias—the more explicit the prompt, the more the model tends to differentiate its treatment.
- The prompting method that most closely resembles real dialogue history depends on the specific dataset and demographic variables—there is no universal "best" method.
- The validity of using names as proxies faces ethical and methodological issues, as a single name can span multiple demographic dimensions.
- Explicit mentions in system prompts vs. user prompts produce different effects—bias is generally larger when mentions are in the system prompt.

## Highlights & Insights

- This is a significant methodological contribution, revealing an overlooked "hidden degree of freedom" in personalization bias research.
- The "more explicit, more biased" finding has practical implications: researchers using high ecological validity (implicit) methods might underestimate bias, while those using low validity (explicit) methods might overestimate it.
- The flexible evaluation framework design can be directly reused by other researchers.

## Limitations & Future Work

- Only covers English.
- Limited persona dimensions (Gender, Race, Age); intersectional identities were not tested.
- Benchmarking data for dialogue history is limited.
- Mitigation strategies were not explored.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs. Kearney et al.**: Provides real interaction benchmark data; this paper compares synthetic prompting methods based on that foundation.
- **vs. Durmus et al.**: Found differences between language vs. explicit mentions; this paper extends to more prompt types and models.
