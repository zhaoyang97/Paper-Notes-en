# FairI Tales: Evaluation of Fairness in Indian Contexts with a Focus on Bias and Stereotypes

**Conference**: ACL 2025  
**arXiv**: [2506.23111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.23111)  
**Code**: [https://github.com/AI4Bharat/indic-bias](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/indic-bias)  
**Area**: AI Safety / Fairness  
**Keywords**: Fairness, Bias Detection, Stereotypes, LLM Evaluation, Indian Society

## TL;DR
This paper propose Indic-Bias, the first large-scale LLM fairness benchmark tailored to the diverse Indian society. Testing 14 LLMs across three evaluation tasks using 20,000 human-verified scenario templates, it reveals that models possess severe negative biases against marginalized groups such as Dalits and reinforce stereotypes in over 70% of the cases.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Fairness research in LLMs has become a crucial topic in the NLP community, with benchmarks such as BBQ, CrowS-Pairs, and WinoBias driving the quantification of gender, racial, and religious biases. However, almost all these studies focus exclusively on Western demographic structures.

**Limitations of Prior Work**:
   - Existing fairness benchmarks are primarily contextualized in Western settings (e.g., White/Black, male/female) and fail to capture unique axes of discrimination in India, such as caste, tribe, and regional identity.
   - The few studies targeting the Indian context (e.g., Indic-Bhed) only cover four identity groups, lacking systematic rigor.
   - Most bias scenarios are automatically generated by LLMs, lacking validation from sociological experts.

**Key Challenge**: India comprises over 80 religions, 730+ tribes, and a highly complex caste system, making it one of the most diverse societies globally. However, LLM fairness research severely under-represents this context, creating a massive evaluation blind spot.

**Goal**: (1) Build a comprehensive benchmark covering 85 Indian identity groups across four axes of discrimination. (2) Design three complementary evaluation tasks to thoroughly probe LLM biases. (3) Systematically evaluate the fairness performance of 14 mainstream LLMs.

**Key Insight**: Starting from participatory research led by sociology experts, a taxonomy of over 1,800 topics is first constructed, followed by the generation of scenario templates. This ensures that the test scenarios genuinely reflect Indian social realities rather than the hallucinations or self-reflections of LLMs.

**Core Idea**: Build a large-scale Indian context fairness benchmark through expert curation and human verification, exposing LLM biases against Indian identity groups across three distinct dimensions (plausibility, judgment, and generation).

## Method

### Overall Architecture
Identity Selection (85 groups × 4 axes of discrimination) → Expert construction of a socio-cultural topic taxonomy (1,800+ topics) → Generation & human verification of 20,000 scenario templates → Three evaluation tasks (Plausibility / Judgment / Generation) → ELO rating system to rank bias levels across different identity groups → Evaluation of 14 LLMs

### Key Designs

1. **Identity System spanning 85 Groups across 4 Axes**:
    - 12 Religions (Hinduism, Islam, Sikhism, etc.)
    - 24 Castes (Brahmins, Dalits, OBC, etc.)
    - 30 Regions (Punjabis, Tamils, Bengalis, etc.)
    - 19 Tribes (Gonds, Santals, Bhils, etc.)
    - Design Motivation: Covering the primary dimensions of bias and discrimination in Indian society, far exceeding prior studies that limited their scope to just 4-7 groups.

2. **Two-Tiered Taxonomy**:
    - Bias classification: Individual level (workplace behavior/personality traits) → Community level (social interaction) → Societal level (crime/justice).
    - Stereotype classification: Organized hierarchically across three levels: Identity Category × Societal Theme × Specific Topic.
    - Validated by 20 annotators from diverse backgrounds and 5 sociology experts.
    - Design Motivation: Ensuring that the scenarios cover authentic, real-world social bias contexts rather than artificial, synthetic tests.

3. **Three Complementary Evaluation Tasks**:
    - **Plausibility**: Presentation of two scenarios identical setup except for the identity change, prompting the model to decide which is more likely to occur—detecting whether the model associates certain identities more frequently with negative events.
    - **Judgment**: Presentation of an ambiguous scenario involving two identities, requiring the model to judge who is the perpetrator or the hero—detecting systematic preferences in decision-making.
    - **Generation**: Prompting the model to offer advice or recommendations for different identities and using an LLM-as-judge to evaluate disparities in quality—detecting allocational harms.
    - Design Motivation: Comprehensive detection spanning three progressive levels: "cognitive bias → decision-making bias → output bias".

### Evaluation Methods
- ELO Rating System: Converts head-to-head pairwise identity comparison results into rankings to identify which groups are consistently associated with negative attributes.
- LLM-as-judge: Uses an auxiliary LLM in the Generation task to assess the disparity in output quality.
- Distinction between positive and negative scenarios: Bias direction is calculated separately for positive and negative scenarios.

### Evaluation Scale

### Key Findings
- **Systematic negative bias against marginalized groups**: Dalits consistently rank at the bottom of the ELO standings, being frequently associated with negative scenarios by the models.
- **Explicit reasoning does not mitigate bias**: Allowing models to rationalize before answering does not improve bias metrics and sometimes even worsens them.
- **Allocational harms in Generation tasks**: Models provide more detailed, empathetic, and personalized advice for certain identity groups, while offering generic, low-quality responses to others.
- **Extremely high stereotype reinforcement rate**: In ambiguous scenarios containing implicit stereotypes, some models reinforce prevalent stereotypes over 70% of the time.
- **Model scale is not a silver bullet**: Larger models perform better on certain metrics, but overall still exhibit significant biases.

## Highlights & Insights
- **Scale and coverage far exceed prior work**: 85 identities × 4 axes × 20K scenarios construct the most comprehensive Indian NLP fairness benchmark to date. For comparison, Indic-Bhed covers only 4 identities and CrowS-Pairs-India covers only 7.
- **Establishment of a sociologist-led taxonomy ensures socio-cultural realism**: This is a major methodological contribution to fairness benchmark creation, avoiding the echo-chamber issue of LLM-generated scenarios.
- **Three-task design offers methodological reference for other cultural contexts**: The progressive detection framework (Plausibility → Judgment → Generation) can be seamlessly migrated to fairness research in other regions such as the Middle East or East Asia.

## Limitations & Future Work
- **English-only prompts**: Although India has 22 official languages, the prompts are currently limited to English, which might underestimate bias in native Indic languages.
- **ELO rating reliance on pairwise comparisons**: Exhaustive pairwise testing of all 85 groups is computationally expensive; hence, only a subset of combinations was evaluated.
- **Intersectionality is not covered**: Intersectionality of multiple identities (e.g., "Dalit + Female") remains unexplored.
- **Lack of concrete bias mitigation strategies**: This study is primarily diagnostic and does not propose targeted debiasing solutions.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs CrowS-Pairs (Nangia et al., 2020)**: CrowS focuses on 9 Western bias dimensions, whereas this work addresses unique Indian dimensions such as caste and tribe, making them complementary.
- **vs Indic-Bhed (Khandelwal et al., 2024)**: Indic-Bhed covers only 4 groups and focuses strictly on stereotype testing, while this work evaluates 85 groups across both bias and stereotype axes.
- **vs BBQ (Parrish et al., 2021)**: BBQ's dual-condition disambiguated/ambiguous design methodology can be combined with the Plausibility task format used in this study.
