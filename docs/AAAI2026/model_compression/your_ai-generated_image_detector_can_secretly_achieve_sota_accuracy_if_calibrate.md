# Your AI-Generated Image Detector Can Secretly Achieve SOTA Accuracy, If Calibrated

**Conference**: AAAI 2026
**arXiv**: [2602.01973](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01973)
**Code**: [github.com/muliyangm/AIGI-Det-Calib](https://github.com/muliyangm/AIGI-Det-Calib)
**Area**: Model Compression
**Keywords**: AI-generated image detection, post-hoc calibration, Bayesian decision theory, distribution shift, decision boundary

## TL;DR

A lightweight post-hoc calibration framework grounded in Bayesian decision theory is proposed. By adding a learnable scalar offset α to the output logits of an existing detector, the method significantly improves detection accuracy under distribution shift without any retraining.

## Background & Motivation

AI-generated image detection is a core task in digital forensics and information security. Existing detectors perform well within their training distribution but degrade sharply when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) forgeries. The authors identify a critical phenomenon: **even on class-balanced test sets, existing detectors systematically tend to misclassify fake images as real**.

The root cause of this phenomenon is:

**"Lazy" decision mechanism**: Models over-rely on surface-level artifacts during training (e.g., frequency noise, edge inconsistencies) that are absent in unseen generative methods.

**Dual sources of distribution shift**:
   - **Label prior shift**: The marginal distributions of real and fake images may differ between training and testing, i.e., $P_{\text{tr}}(y) \neq P_{\text{te}}(y)$
   - **Class-conditional input shift**: The conditional distribution of fake images $P_{\text{tr}}(x|y=1) \neq P_{\text{te}}(x|y=1)$ changes across generative methods

**Decision boundary misalignment**: The above shifts cause the model's default threshold $f(x)=0$ to no longer be Bayes-optimal.

The authors clearly illustrate this phenomenon via logit distribution visualizations (Figure 1): for CNNSpot trained on ProGAN, the logit distributions of fake images generated by StyleGAN2, WFIR, and Midjourney shift entirely to the wrong side of the decision boundary.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

The proposed method is a **post-hoc calibration framework** that leaves all parameters of the original detector unchanged. At inference time, a scalar calibration value α is added to the model's output logits. The core pipeline is:
1. Extract logit values from test samples using the pretrained detector.
2. Optimize the calibration parameter α using a small set of validation samples (default: 100 images, ~1% of the test set).
3. Use the calibrated logit $\tilde{f}(x) = f(x) - \alpha$ for final prediction.

### Key Designs

#### 1. **Proof of Bayes Sub-optimality (Proposition 1)**

Via Bayes' theorem, the logit output under the training distribution is:

$$f(x) = \log\frac{P_{\text{tr}}(x|y=1) P_{\text{tr}}(y=1)}{P_{\text{tr}}(x|y=0) P_{\text{tr}}(y=0)}$$

Under the test distribution, the Bayes-optimal decision boundary requires:

$$f(x) + \Delta(x) = 0$$

where the shift term is:

$$\Delta(x) = \log\frac{P_{\text{te}}(x|1)}{P_{\text{tr}}(x|1)} + \log\frac{P_{\text{te}}(1)(1-P_{\text{tr}}(1))}{P_{\text{tr}}(1)(1-P_{\text{te}}(1))}$$

Assuming the real image distribution remains stable across training and testing ($P_{\text{te}}(x|0) \approx P_{\text{tr}}(x|0)$), the shift originates primarily from the fake image class. Whenever $\Delta(x) \neq 0$, the default threshold is no longer Bayes-optimal.

#### 2. **Theoretical Basis for Scalar Correction (Proposition 2)**

Two key assumptions are made:
- **Assumption 1 (Systematic conditional shift)**: Fake images produced by different generative models exhibit consistent and systematic visual deviations, so the log-likelihood ratio for test fake images can be approximated as a constant $c$.
- **Assumption 2 (Uniform prior shift)**: The prior shift of fake samples in the test set is a constant $\delta$.

Under these two assumptions, $\Delta(x) \approx c + \delta' = \text{const}$, and thus a global scalar $\tilde{\alpha} = -(c+\delta')$ suffices to correct the decision boundary. The calibrated logit is:

$$\tilde{f}(x) = f(x) - \tilde{\alpha}$$

#### 3. **Supervised Calibration**

When a small amount of labeled target data is available, kernel density estimation (KDE) is used to model the logit distributions of both classes:

$$p_0(z) = p(z|y=0), \quad p_1(z) = p(z|y=1)$$

The optimal α is obtained by minimizing the classification error:

$$\alpha^{\star} = \arg\min_{\alpha} \left[\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha} p_1(z)dz + \int_{\alpha}^{\infty} p_0(z)dz\right]$$

#### 4. **Unsupervised Calibration**

Without labels, the method exploits the inherent bimodal structure of the logit distribution. KDE is used to estimate the density $p(z)$, and the calibration point that renders the distribution symmetric is sought:

$$\Phi(\alpha) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (z-\alpha) \cdot p(z) dz = 0$$

When $\Phi(\alpha)=0$, the distribution is symmetric about α, which serves as the natural decision boundary.

### Loss & Training

- The method **requires no training or fine-tuning**; only the scalar α is optimized at inference time.
- The supervised variant optimizes α via KDE and accuracy maximization.
- The unsupervised variant employs a moment-balancing procedure to ensure robustness.
- By default, only 100 validation samples (~1% of data) are required; as few as 10 samples (<0.1%) also yield effective calibration.

## Key Experimental Results

### Key Findings

1. **Calibration yields the largest gains for strong feature extractors (CLIP-based)**: RINE and Effort improve by over 16% and 5% on GenImage, respectively.
2. **Unsupervised calibration closely matches supervised calibration**: The gap is within 1–2% in most cases.
3. **Strong robustness to image perturbations**: Calibration gains are even larger under JPEG compression and Gaussian blur.
4. **Effective calibration with very few samples**: As few as 10 images suffice to achieve the majority of the performance improvement.

## Highlights & Insights

1. **Precise problem diagnosis**: Starting from Bayesian decision theory, the paper rigorously proves why the default threshold is suboptimal under distribution shift, which is more compelling than purely empirical observations.
2. **Extremely lightweight solution**: A single scalar parameter requires no retraining, no access to training data, and no knowledge of the model's internal structure — it is plug-and-play for any detector.
3. **Strong alignment between theory and practice**: The two assumptions (systematic conditional shift and uniform prior shift) are approximations, yet are thoroughly validated through experiments.
4. **Practical value of the unsupervised variant**: In real-world deployment scenarios without labeled data, unsupervised calibration still yields substantial improvements.

## Limitations & Future Work

1. **Scope of the assumptions**: The systematic conditional shift assumption requires that images from the same generator exhibit consistent offsets; a single scalar may be insufficient when the test set contains multiple highly diverse generators.
2. **Binary classification only**: The method currently handles only real/fake binary classification and has not been extended to generator attribution tasks.
3. **Highly overlapping distributions**: When the logit distributions of real and fake images overlap heavily, the effectiveness of a scalar offset is limited.
4. Future work could explore **region-wise or generator-adaptive calibration**, or extend the scalar to an input-dependent function.

## Related Work & Insights

- **Logit adjustment** methods (Menon et al., 2020) in long-tailed learning provide theoretical inspiration for this work.
- The approach bears resemblance to **bias correction methods in continual learning**, but addresses distribution shift across generative models.
- Takeaway: Many seemingly complex problems may be substantially alleviated by simple yet theoretically grounded post-processing strategies.
