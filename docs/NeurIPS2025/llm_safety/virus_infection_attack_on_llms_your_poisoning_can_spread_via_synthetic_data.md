# Virus Infection Attack on LLMs: Your Poisoning Can Spread "VIA" Synthetic Data

**Conference**: NeurIPS 2025
**arXiv**: [2509.23041](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23041)  
**Code**: [GitHub](https://github.com/liangzid/VirusInfectionAttack)  
**Area**: AI Security
**Keywords**: data poisoning, synthetic data, backdoor attack, LLM security, poisoning propagation

## TL;DR

This paper presents the first systematic study of security risks introduced by synthetic data in LLM training. It reveals that existing poisoning and backdoor attacks rarely propagate through synthetic data, and proposes the Virus Infection Attack (VIA) framework. VIA embeds poisoned content into normal training samples via hijacking point search and shell construction, enabling malicious content to be generated by the model even on clean queries and subsequently propagated to downstream models.

## Background & Motivation

Synthetic data is widely used across all stages of LLM development — pretraining, SFT, RLHF, and distillation — and has been shown to substantially improve reasoning and instruction-following capabilities. However, the paradigm of "upstream model generates synthetic data → downstream model trains on it" harbors an overlooked security risk: **can poisoned content in an upstream model propagate to downstream models through synthetic data?**

The authors first conduct a systematic evaluation of mainstream poisoning and backdoor attacks, uncovering a critical phenomenon: **existing attacks almost never propagate through synthetic data**. Specifically, even when the upstream model's attack success rate (ASR) is high, the infection rate (IR) in the generated synthetic data remains consistently below 0.1%.

Through analysis of over 4.3 million text queries, the authors identify the root cause: poisoned content and backdoor triggers typically concentrate in an extremely narrow subspace of the query space. For example, across three SFT datasets, the proportions of poisoning-related queries are only 0.09%, 0.23%, 0.24%, and 0.00%, respectively. Consequently, when a maintainer uses general-purpose queries to generate synthetic data, poisoning behaviors are almost never triggered. This **distributional decoupling** is the fundamental reason existing attacks fail to propagate.

This motivates a key question: can an attack be designed with enhanced propagation capability through synthetic data, and if so, how can it be defended against?

## Method

### Overall Architecture

VIA draws inspiration from the propagation mechanism of computer viruses. Unlike conventional poisoning attacks that inject malicious samples as standalone training data, VIA embeds poisoned content (payload) into normal training samples through two key steps: (1) Hijacking Point Search (HPS) to identify the optimal injection site; and (2) Shell Construction (SC) to wrap the payload in a protective shell to improve stealthiness.

### Key Designs

1. **Formal modeling of infectious poisoning**: Given an SFT dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(Q_i,R_i)\}$, a poisoning text $P$ is injected at rate $\rho$ into modified responses $\tilde{R}=R_l||R_c||f_s(P)||R_r$, where $R_c$ is the hijacking anchor and $f_s$ is the wrapping function. The optimization objective consists of three terms: maximizing the occurrence rate of the payload in synthetic data, preserving the training objective, and suppressing the generation probability of clean responses. The authors derive a lower-bound search objective (Equation 2) that decomposes the infection rate into the product of three factors: the effect of the hijacking point $\frac{1}{\mathbf{P}_\theta(R_r|Q,R_l,R_c)}$, the effect of the shell $\mathbf{P}_{\tilde\theta}(\tilde{P}|Q,R_l,R_c)$, and the influence on final generation.

2. **Hijacking Point Search (HPS)**: A scoring function $S_{R_c} = \log N_{R_c} - \log \max_{R_r} N_{R_r}$ is designed, where $N_{R_c}$ and $N_{R_r}$ denote the occurrence frequencies of $R_c$ and $R_c||R_r$ in the dataset, respectively. The core idea is to identify n-gram segments that **appear frequently in the training corpus but exhibit high uncertainty in subsequent text**. Such segments serve as "branching points" during generation and are thus susceptible to hijacking. The authors analyze the HPS score distribution of the top-50 high-frequency 3-grams in commonly used SFT datasets and identify several vulnerable patterns. By default, 3-grams or 4-grams are used as hijacking points.

3. **Shell Construction (SC)**: Two wrapping strategies are proposed: (a) a fixed-format strategy that surrounds the payload with quotation marks; and (b) an LLM-generation strategy that prompts an LLM to produce two bridging texts $P_{pre}$ and $P_{suf}$, enabling the wrapped payload $\tilde{P} = P_{pre}||P||P_{suf}$ to blend naturally with its context. Additionally, a Semantic Similarity search (SS) strategy is introduced to re-rank candidate training samples by their semantic relevance to the payload, further improving stealthiness.

### Loss & Training

- **Serialization mode**: For poisoned samples in dialogue format, VIA serializes them into plain text.
- **Mixup strategy (VIA mixup)**: Half of the poisoned samples are used directly as training data, while the other half are embedded via VIA, jointly ensuring high upstream ASR and downstream IR.
- **Poisoning rate**: Set to 2% by default, using LLaMA-3 8B as the backbone model.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results — Data Poisoning

| Attack Method | Scenario | ASR-Upstream | IR-Synthetic Data | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CoT/Response Poisoning | Sentiment Steering | 100.00% | 0.20% | Traditional methods yield near-zero IR |
| VIA (HPS 4-gram) | Sentiment Steering | 53.65% | **85.64%** | Substantial IR improvement |
| CoT/Response Poisoning | Knowledge Injection | 100.00% | 0.22% | Traditional methods yield near-zero IR |
| VIA (HPS 4-gram) | Knowledge Injection | 94.74% | **62.38%** | 300× IR improvement |
| CoT/Response Poisoning | Biased Recommendation | 5.26% | 0.06% | Traditional methods nearly ineffective |
| VIA (HPS 4-gram) | Biased Recommendation | 68.42% | **87.82%** | Breakthrough improvement |

|---|---|---|---|
| BadNet | 85.86/0.05 | 99.50/0.15 | 100.00/0.02 |
| BadNet+VIA(mixup) | 77.40/**46.37** | 100.00/**70.82** | 100.00/**78.72** |
| CTBA | 89.90/0.12 | 100.00/0.45 | 99.50/0.40 |

### Ablation Study — Stealthiness Analysis (PPL Detection)

| Strategy | 3-gram Detection Recall↓ | 5-gram Detection Recall↓ | 7-gram Detection Recall↓ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random Insertion | 87.20% | 72.40% | 40.80% |
| HPS | 45.60% | 42.80% | 19.60% |
| HPS + SC | **29.20%** | **30.00%** | **11.60%** |
| HPS + SS + SC | **33.20%** | **27.60%** | **10.00%** |

### Key Findings

- The propagation rate of existing poisoning methods through synthetic data is near zero (IR < 0.1%), indicating that the synthetic data training paradigm exhibits natural resistance to conventional attacks.
- VIA raises IR from near zero to 50–87%, demonstrating that synthetic data is not unconditionally safe.
- The VIA mixup strategy can simultaneously maintain high upstream ASR and downstream IR.
- The HPS and SC modules reduce the recall of PPL-based detection from 87% to 29%, significantly improving stealthiness.

## Highlights & Insights

- **Unique research perspective**: This is the first work to systematically examine synthetic data from a security standpoint within the LLM development pipeline, filling an important research gap.
- **Elegant virus metaphor**: The propagation mechanism of computer viruses (payload + shell + injection point) is elegantly mapped onto the LLM poisoning scenario, yielding a design that is both principled and effective.
- **Dual validation via theory and experiment**: The failure of existing attacks is explained through distributional analysis, and an actionable attack enhancement scheme is provided.

## Limitations & Future Work

- VIA improves IR at the cost of reducing upstream ASR; while the mixup strategy partially mitigates this trade-off, it does not resolve it fundamentally.
- Only LLaMA-3 8B is used as the backbone model; performance on larger-scale models remains unknown.
- The proposed defense strategy (PPL filtering) is relatively preliminary and lacks a more systematic defensive framework.
- The decoupled optimization strategy may not yield a globally optimal solution.

## Related Work & Insights

- The work contrasts with classical literature on data poisoning (Gu et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2024) and backdoor attacks (BackdoorLLM).
- It offers a new auditing perspective for the safe use of synthetic data, serving as a warning for training pipelines that rely heavily on synthetic data, such as those used for DeepSeek.
- It extends the concept of "supply chain attacks" to the AI domain.
