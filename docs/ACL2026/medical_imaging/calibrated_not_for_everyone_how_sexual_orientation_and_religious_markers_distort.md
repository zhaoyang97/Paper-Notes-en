# Calibrated? Not for Everyone: How Sexual Orientation and Religious Markers Distort LLM Accuracy and Confidence in Medical QA

**Conference**: ACL 2026
**arXiv**: [2604.17316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17316)
**Code**: None
**Area**: AI Fairness / Medical NLP
**Keywords**: Calibration Bias, Social Identity Markers, Medical QA, Uncertainty Estimation, Intersectional Identity

## TL;DR

This paper investigates how social identity markers (sexual orientation and religious affiliation) distort LLM accuracy and confidence calibration in medical question answering. It finds that the "homosexual" marker consistently degrades performance and induces calibration crises across 9 LLMs, and that intersectional identities produce non-additive, identity-specific harms.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: LLMs are increasingly integrated into clinical workflows (patient communication, decision support), and clinical systems routinely rely on model confidence scores to triage cases, trigger escalations, or hand off to clinicians. Safe deployment therefore requires not only high accuracy but also robust uncertainty calibration.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Prior studies have shown that social descriptors (e.g., race, gender) alter LLM clinical recommendations, yet no work has assessed how identity markers affect model uncertainty. This blind spot is particularly dangerous in clinical settings—if identity cues systematically distort confidence signals, the result is inequitable patient triage.

**Key Challenge**: Social identity information that carries no diagnostic value should not influence medical reasoning; however, LLMs may have learned bias patterns associated with such identities from training data, causing simultaneous degradation of both accuracy and calibration.

**Goal**: To systematically quantify the effects of sexual orientation and religious affiliation markers on LLM medical QA performance and semantic entropy calibration.

**Key Insight**: A counterfactual approach is adopted—different identity marker sentences are inserted into identical clinical vignettes, and changes in model behavior are measured comparatively.

**Core Idea**: Identity markers do not merely shift the predictive distribution; they undermine the reliability of confidence signals. A "calibration crisis" poses greater risks than accuracy degradation alone.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

2,364 medical questions are randomly sampled from MedQA-USMLE. Counterfactual variants are generated by inserting template sentences encoding sexual orientation and/or religious affiliation. QA accuracy and semantic entropy calibration are then evaluated across 9 LLMs.

### Key Designs

1. **Counterfactual Variant Construction**:

    - **Function**: Generate medical question variants that differ only in identity markers.
    - **Mechanism**: A template sentence—e.g., "The patient identifies as heterosexual/homosexual" and/or "The patient is Catholic/Muslim/atheist"—is inserted before the final sentence of each clinical vignette. Questions already containing sexual orientation, religious, or psychiatric content are excluded. This yields 8 variant types: +hetero, +homo, +Cat, +Mus, +Ath, +homo+Cat, +homo+Mus, +homo+Ath.
    - **Design Motivation**: Ensures that observed differences are attributable solely to identity markers, with all other conditions held constant.

2. **Semantic Entropy Calibration Evaluation**:

    - **Function**: Quantify changes in the reliability of model uncertainty across different identity conditions.
    - **Mechanism**: Semantic Entropy is used as the uncertainty measure—predictive uncertainty is quantified over semantically equivalent output classes rather than surface forms. Calibration quality is assessed via the Brier score to detect whether identity markers cause the model to express high confidence on incorrect predictions.
    - **Design Motivation**: Semantic entropy better reflects genuine model uncertainty than raw output probabilities and has demonstrated superior performance in clinical validation settings.

3. **Intersectional Identity Effect Analysis**:

    - **Function**: Detect whether combined identity markers produce harms exceeding the additive effects of individual markers.
    - **Mechanism**: The effects of single markers (e.g., +homo) are compared with those of combined markers (e.g., +homo+Muslim). If the combined effect exceeds the sum of the two individual effects, non-additive intersectional harm is confirmed.
    - **Design Motivation**: Real patients hold multiple simultaneous identities; intersectional analyses more accurately approximate real-world risk than single-dimension evaluations.

### Loss & Training

This is a purely evaluative study; no model training is involved.

### Key Findings

- The "heterosexual" marker approximates a neutral baseline, whereas the "homosexual" marker consistently triggers accuracy drops and calibration deterioration across all 9 LLMs.
- Intersectional identities produce non-additive harms: the effect of +homo+Catholic frequently exceeds the sum of the individual effects of +homo and +Catholic.
- Specialized biomedical models (Bio-Medical-Llama, OpenBioLLM) exhibit greater bias than general-purpose models.
- Identical patterns are confirmed in open-ended generation settings, ruling out artifacts of the multiple-choice format.
- Even the strongest model, GPT-5.1, is affected, albeit to a lesser degree.

## Highlights & Insights

- The concept of a "calibration crisis" is particularly significant: in clinical settings, a high-confidence incorrect answer is far more dangerous than a low-confidence incorrect answer. Identity markers compromise not only accuracy but also the reliability of confidence signals.
- The finding that specialized biomedical models exhibit greater bias is counterintuitive—likely because biomedical fine-tuning corpora themselves contain stronger identity-associated bias patterns.
- The use of semantic entropy rather than simple probabilities to measure uncertainty represents a methodological strength that enhances the credibility of the results.

## Limitations & Future Work

- Identity markers cover only 3 religious affiliations and 2 sexual orientations; broader identity coverage is a natural future direction.
- Identity information is inserted via template sentences, whereas real clinical records present such information in far more diverse and implicit ways.
- Only English-language USMLE questions are evaluated; bias patterns may differ across other languages and healthcare systems.
- No mitigation strategies are proposed—how to eliminate identity bias while preserving clinical accuracy remains an open problem.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs. Ji et al. (2025)**: Examines the impact of sociodemographic attributes on clinical trial matching but does not evaluate uncertainty calibration; this paper is the first to introduce calibration analysis into bias research.
- **vs. Hirsch et al. (2026)**: Studies LGBTQIA+ bias but not in clinical settings; this paper focuses on concrete safety risks in medical QA.
- **vs. Schmidgall et al. (2024)**: Investigates the effects of cognitive bias on LLMs but does not involve identity markers; this paper focuses specifically on systematic bias induced by social identity.
