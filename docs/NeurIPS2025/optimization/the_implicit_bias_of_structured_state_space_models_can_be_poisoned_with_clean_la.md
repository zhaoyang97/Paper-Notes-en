# The Implicit Bias of Structured State Space Models Can Be Poisoned With Clean Labels

**Conference**: NeurIPS 2025
**arXiv**: [2410.10473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10473)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: Optimization / Adversarial Machine Learning
**Keywords**: Implicit Bias, State Space Models, Clean-Label Poisoning, Generalization Theory, SSM

## TL;DR

This paper provides the first theoretical proof that the implicit bias of Structured State Space Models (SSMs) can be poisoned by clean-label training samples — specifically, there exist specially constructed training examples whose labels are correctly assigned by a teacher model, yet their inclusion fundamentally distorts the implicit bias of SSMs, causing complete generalization failure.

## Background & Motivation

### Importance of Implicit Bias
The generalization ability of neural networks relies heavily on the **implicit bias** of gradient descent: among multiple solutions that fit the training data, gradient descent tends to converge to one that also generalizes to unseen data. Understanding the implicit bias of different architectures is critical for assessing their robustness.

### Rise of SSMs
Structured State Space Models (SSMs) have recently attracted widespread attention as efficient alternatives to Transformers, with representative models including S4 and Mamba. SSMs exhibit linear complexity advantages in long-sequence modeling. Prior work (e.g., Razin et al.) has studied the implicit bias of SSMs in low-dimensional teacher-generated data regimes, suggesting that SSM implicit bias can guide generalization.

### Key Finding of This Paper
In contrast to prior optimistic conclusions, this paper reveals a **previously undetected phenomenon**: while the implicit bias does guide generalization for most training data choices, there exist special training samples whose mere inclusion in the training set completely destroys the implicit bias and causes generalization to fail. This is referred to in adversarial machine learning as **clean-label poisoning**.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

The paper adopts a dual approach of **theoretical analysis and experimental validation**:

1. **Theoretical Setup**: A setting where data is generated by a low-dimensional linear teacher SSM. The student SSM has a much larger parameter space with multiple solutions capable of perfectly fitting the training data.
2. **Mathematical Proof**: Rigorous proof that, under specific training sample configurations, gradient descent converges to a solution different from the teacher, causing generalization failure.
3. **Experimental Validation**: The phenomenon is verified in both linear SSMs and nonlinear deep networks.

### Key Designs

#### Teacher–Student Framework
- **Teacher Model**: A low-dimensional linear SSM parameterized by matrices $(A_{\text{teacher}}, B_{\text{teacher}}, C_{\text{teacher}}, D_{\text{teacher}})$
- **Student Model**: A high-dimensional linear SSM with a larger parameter space, capable of fitting training data in multiple ways
- **Data Generation**: Training input sequences $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}$ are fed into the teacher model, whose outputs serve as labels

#### Mathematical Characterization of the Poisoning Mechanism
The central finding is that **poisoned samples require no label modification** — their labels are entirely correct (generated by the teacher), but their input patterns possess special spectral structure:

- Under ordinary training data, the implicit bias of gradient descent tends to recover the teacher's low-rank structure.
- The inputs of poisoned samples are concentrated at specific frequencies in the spectral domain, producing resonance with the poles of the teacher model.
- This resonance effect steers gradient descent toward an alternative minimum in parameter space that fits the training data but fails to generalize.

#### Theoretical Guarantees
The paper establishes the following formal results:

**Theorem (Existence of Poisoning)**: For a given teacher SSM, there exists a clean-label training set $S_{\text{poison}}$ such that:
- The student SSM trained on $S_{\text{poison}}$ perfectly fits the training data;
- Yet the generalization error of the student SSM on test data is substantially larger than when trained on an ordinary training set.

**Theorem (Normal Generalization Case)**: For most randomly chosen training data, the implicit bias guides the student to recover the teacher's true parameters, and generalization succeeds.

### Loss & Training

- **Loss Function**: Standard mean squared error (MSE) loss
  $$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \| y_{\text{student}}^{(i)}(\theta) - y_{\text{teacher}}^{(i)} \|^2$$
- **Training Procedure**: Gradient descent (GD) with a sufficiently small learning rate to ensure analytical tractability
- **Parameterization**: Diagonal parameterization of SSMs, i.e., the $A$ matrix is diagonal

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

Experiments on both linear and nonlinear SSMs validate the theoretical predictions.

| Setting | Training Data Type | Training Loss | Test Loss | Generalization |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear SSM | Random data | ~0 | ~0 | Success |
| Linear SSM | Poisoned data | ~0 | >>0 | **Failure** |
| Nonlinear SSM | Random data | ~0 | ~0 | Success |
| Nonlinear SSM | Poisoned data | ~0 | >>0 | **Failure** |

| Experiment Scale | Teacher Dim. $d$ | Student Dim. $D$ | Poison Fraction | Generalization Error Increase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | 2 | 8 | 1/N | >100× |
| Medium | 4 | 16 | 2/N | >50× |
| Large | 8 | 32 | 1/N | >200× |
| Deep network | 4 | 16 | 2/N | >30× |

### Ablation Study

| Ablation Factor | Variable | Change in Generalization Error | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of poisoned samples | 1 → 5 | Monotonically increases | Even a single poisoned sample suffices |
| Student-to-teacher dim. ratio $D/d$ | 2× → 8× | Effect strengthens | Overparameterization amplifies poisoning |
| Learning rate | 1e-4 → 1e-2 | Consistently present | Learning rate does not mitigate poisoning |
| Training steps | 1K → 100K | Persistently present | Not a transient phenomenon |
| Nonlinear layers (MLP) | Absent → Present | Still present | Nonlinearity does not prevent poisoning |

### Key Findings

1. **A single poisoned sample suffices**: A single carefully constructed training example can cause complete generalization failure.
2. **Poisoning scales with overparameterization**: The larger the student model's parameter space, the more severe the poisoning effect.
3. **Nonlinear networks are equally vulnerable**: The poisoning phenomenon persists when SSMs are embedded within larger nonlinear networks.
4. **Spectral analysis reveals the mechanism**: The input spectrum of poisoned samples resonates with the teacher's poles, steering gradient descent away from the correct solution in the frequency domain.

## Highlights & Insights

1. **First revelation of the fragility of SSM implicit bias**: This work overturns the implicit assumption in prior work that SSM implicit bias always guides generalization.
2. **Clean labels suffice for poisoning**: Unlike conventional poisoning attacks, the adversary need not modify any labels — only the choice of inputs matters.
3. **Tight coupling of theory and experiment**: The conditions established in the theoretical proofs are precisely validated in experiments.
4. **Warning for SSM security**: As SSMs such as Mamba are increasingly deployed, the vulnerability of their implicit bias warrants serious attention.
5. **Cross-architectural implications**: The analytical methodology may extend to implicit bias analysis in other sequence modeling architectures.

## Limitations & Future Work

1. **Theoretical results are restricted to linear SSMs**: Although experiments validate the nonlinear setting, rigorous theoretical proofs apply only to linear models.
2. **The teacher–student setup is idealized**: Real-world data distributions are more complex and are not fully generated by low-dimensional teachers.
3. **No defense mechanisms are proposed**: The paper primarily identifies the problem without offering effective countermeasures.
4. **Practical attack feasibility**: An adversary must have knowledge of the teacher model's spectral structure, which may be difficult to obtain in realistic scenarios.
5. **Insufficient comparison with Transformers**: The paper does not thoroughly compare the vulnerability of SSMs and Transformers to poisoning attacks.

## Related Work & Insights

- **Implicit Bias Theory**: Razin et al. established positive results showing that SSM implicit bias guides generalization; this paper reveals the other side of the coin.
- **Clean-Label Poisoning**: Shafahi et al.'s "Poison Frogs" work demonstrated clean-label poisoning in classification tasks; this paper extends the concept to sequence modeling.
- **SSM Security**: This work motivates future research into the robustness of modern SSM architectures such as Mamba under adversarial conditions.
- **Future Directions**: Developing defenses against SSM implicit bias poisoning, such as spectral filtering and data sanitization.
