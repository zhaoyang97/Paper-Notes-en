# Identifiability Challenges in Sparse Linear Ordinary Differential Equations

**Conference**: ICLR 2026  
**OpenReview**: [https://openreview.net/forum?id=BYBKqpZteT](https://openreview.net/forum?id=BYBKqpZteT)  
**Code**: https://github.com/ceciliacasolo/ode-identifiability  
**Area**: Learning Theory / Dynamical Systems Identifiability  
**Keywords**: Sparse linear ODEs, Identifiability, Dynamical systems, Matrix rank, Near-unidentifiability  

## TL;DR

This paper revisits the classical conclusion that "linear ODEs can almost always be identified from a single trajectory," pointing out that this conclusion only covers dense matrix cases. In sparse linear ODEs that are closer to biological networks, social networks, and physical interaction systems, systems are unidentifiable with positive probability. This theoretical unidentifiability manifests as structural recovery failure in practical estimators such as SINDy and Neural ODEs.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Dynamical system modeling has long been a core tool for scientific modeling. Traditionally, researchers manually wrote differential equations based on domain knowledge; in recent years, with the popularity of machine learning and high-throughput data, more work has shifted towards inferring governing equations from observed trajectories—learning system matrices or dynamic functions from data. In linear, homogeneous, autonomous ODEs $\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)$, existing theoretical results are often summarized as: almost all linear ODEs can be identified from a single complete, noise-free, continuously observed trajectory.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: The claim of being "almost always identifiable" is easily over-cited in practice. Many real-world systems are not dense interaction networks but are highly sparse: only a few genes interact in gene regulatory networks, each individual connects to a tiny fraction of others in social networks, and only local coupling often exists in physical or biological systems. If a learned sparse ODE fits the observed trajectory well, researchers often further interpret the non-zero edges as true relationships between variables. However, if the sparse system itself is unidentifiable, even a perfect trajectory fit does not imply the recovery of the true dynamics.

**Key Challenge**: Classical results rely on the system matrix following a dense distribution that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, whereas the zero-patterns of sparse matrices place probability mass on low-dimensional structures. In other words, the "bad set" of repeated eigenvalues or low rank causing unidentifiability has measure zero in a dense world; in a sparse world, zero columns, zero rows, or more general sparse connection patterns cause such bad structures to appear with positive probability. Sparsity is both an inductive bias required for interpretable modeling and a structural source that destroys unique recovery.

**Goal**: The authors aim to answer a specific question: given a trajectory $x:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^n$ generated by a sparse linear ODE, under what dimension $n$ and sparsity $p$ can the system matrix $A$ be uniquely inferred? If it remains theoretically identifiable, will initial conditions near invariant subspaces cause practical near-unidentifiability? Finally, will these theoretical phenomena be "automatically fixed" by the regularization, optimization processes, or inductive biases of practical algorithms?

**Key Insight**: The paper chooses the most ideal and most discriminative setting: continuous time, noise-free, full-state observation, and a single trajectory. This is not to claim that real-world data is this clean, but to exclude additional difficulties such as sampling, noise, and missing observations; if a sparse system is still unidentifiable in this best-case scenario, then the problem stems from the system structure itself rather than measurement equipment or optimization errors.

**Core Idea**: Use the rank deficiency and invariant subspace structure of sparse random matrices to re-characterize the identifiability boundaries of linear ODEs, and verify through practical estimators that a fundamental gap may exist between "fitting trajectories" and "identifying the true system matrix."

## Method

### Overall Architecture

This paper does not propose a new ODE learning algorithm but builds a theoretical and empirical analysis framework. The input is a class of sparse linear ODEs $\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)$ and an observed trajectory generated by an initial condition $x_0$; the output is not a predicted trajectory, but a determination and probability boundary regarding whether $A$ can be uniquely identified from that trajectory.

The authors first decompose unidentifiability into two levels: system-level unidentifiability focuses on whether the matrix $A$ itself cannot be uniquely recovered regardless of the trajectory; trajectory-level unidentifiability focuses on whether $A$ is theoretically identifiable but a specific initial condition $x_0$ falls in or near an invariant subspace. Subsequently, the paper derives the positive probability lower bounds and asymptotic thresholds for system-level unidentifiability under a sparse-continuous ensemble, then translates near-unidentifiability into error bounds for the indistinguishability of trajectories generated by two candidate systems within a finite time window. Finally, the authors conduct empirical tests using SINDy and sparse Neural ODEs: even if the models only retain samples with good trajectory fits, the sparse structure of the estimated matrices deviates from the true matrix as sparsity and dimension worsen.

### Key Designs

**1. System-level and Trajectory-level Decomposition: Turning "Unidentifiability" into Calculable Probabilities**

The paper first formalizes the problem of identifying linear ODEs from trajectories. If $A\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ and the trajectory satisfies $\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)$, then $A$ is identifiable from $x$ if there exists no other $B\neq A$ that also satisfies $\dot{x}(t)=Bx(t)$. Existing results tell us that $A$ is globally unidentifiable if and only if more than one Jordan block corresponds to an eigenvalue in its Jordan normal form; $A$ is identifiable from a specific trajectory if and only if the initial condition $x(0)$ is not in any $A$-invariant proper subspace.

This decomposition is crucial because it writes the failure probability of "random system + random initial condition" as two terms:

$$
P_{A,x_0}(A\text{ not identifiable from }x_0)
= P_A(U) + \int \chi_{A\notin U}(A)P_{A,x_0}(U_A\mid A)\,dP_A(A).
$$

Here $U$ is the set of globally unidentifiable systems, and $U_A$ is the set of initial conditions that make a given $A$ unidentifiable from a trajectory. If the distribution of $x_0$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, then for any $A$ that is not globally unidentifiable, the probability of a random initial condition falling exactly into an invariant proper subspace is zero. Therefore, the unidentifiability probability in a random sense is mainly determined by $P_A(U)$. This split avoids mixing "unlucky initial conditions" with "no solution for the system itself" and provides an entry point for subsequent sparse matrix analysis.

**2. Sparse-continuous Ensemble: Explaining Why Sparse Systems Are No Longer "Measure Zero" Using Zero-patterns**

The authors define a sparse continuous random matrix as $A=(B_{ij}X_{ij})$, where $B_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(1-p)$ controls whether the position is non-zero, $X_{ij}$ comes from a continuous distribution, and $p$ represents the probability of zero elements—the larger $p$, the more sparse the matrix. This model preserves the continuity of non-zero weights while explicitly modeling sparse zero-patterns, thus revealing the blind spots of classical dense conclusions.

Under this ensemble, the paper first proves that repeated non-zero eigenvalues still appear with probability zero; the main event bringing global unidentifiability shifts to the multiplicity of the $0$ eigenvalue, i.e., the matrix rank being lower than $n-1$. The authors provide an intuitive but powerful lower bound: as long as at least two columns in the Bernoulli zero-pattern are all-zero, the matrix rank is at most $n-2$, triggering global unidentifiability. Thus, for $n\ge 2$:

$$
P(A\in U) \ge 1-(1-p^n)^n-np^n(1-p^n)^{n-1}.
$$

This lower bound does not claim that zero columns are the only source, but shows that sparsity creates rank deficiency with positive probability. The appendix further generalizes the argument to more general structured sparse patterns: as long as a set of columns connects to very few rows, the matrix may exhibit at least a second-order rank deficiency. Consequently, the paper's conclusions are not limited to i.i.d. Bernoulli sparse graphs but also cover networks with hubs or local sparse clusters.

**3. Sparsity Threshold: Pointing to a Phase Transition in Unidentifiability Near $p=1-\log(n)/n$**

A positive lower bound alone is insufficient; the authors further analyze what sparsity level causes global unidentifiability to shift from a rare to a high-probability event as the dimension $n$ increases. The paper maps sparse matrices to random bipartite graphs and characterizes the matrix rank through the relationship between matching numbers and Hall deficiency. The conclusion is that a sharp threshold exists:

$$
p_c(n)=1-\frac{\log n}{n}.
$$

If $p(n)=p_c(n)+\omega(n)/n$ and $\omega(n)\to\infty$, then $P(\mathrm{rank}(A)\le n-2)\to 1$; if $p(n)=p_c(n)-\omega(n)/n$, the probability approaches $0$. Here $p$ is the probability of zero elements, so the closer $p$ is to 1, the sparser the system. The practical meaning of this threshold is: high-dimensional systems are not automatically safe; the key is whether the true number of interactions is below the scale of $n\log n$.

The paper uses statistics from real gene regulatory networks to show that this threshold is not a pure mathematical toy. For example, the P. trichocarpa network has about 1690 nodes and 9268 edges, with a sparsity of about $0.9968$, while the threshold is about $0.9957$; the A. thaliana network has about 2864 nodes and 18663 edges, with a sparsity of about $0.9977$ and a threshold of about $0.9972$. These numbers imply that many highly sparse networks of real interest in scientific modeling likely fall into regions with a high risk of unidentifiability.

**4. Near-unidentifiability: How Zero-probability Unlucky Trajectories Become Practical Problems in Finite Windows**

From a purely probabilistic perspective, the probability of a random initial condition falling exactly into an invariant proper subspace is zero. In practice, however, one does not need to "fall exactly"; being close enough can make two different systems behave almost identically for a finite time. To this end, the paper defines the normalized distance from the initial condition to all $A$-invariant proper subspaces:

$$
d_A(x_0)=\frac{1}{\|x_0\|_2}\min_{V\in I(A)}\mathrm{dist}(x_0,V).
$$

When $d_A(x_0)=0$, trajectory-level unidentifiability truly occurs; when it is small, although the trajectory is theoretically identifiable, distinguishing candidate systems requires longer time, more precise observations, or stronger experimental intervention. The paper proves: if there exists an $A$-invariant subspace $V^*$ and another matrix $A'$ consistent with $A$ on that subspace, i.e., $(A-A')V^*=\{0\}$, then the difference between the trajectories generated by both starting from $x_0$ satisfies:

$$
\|e^{At}x_0-e^{A't}x_0\|_2
\le C(t,A,A')\|A-A'\|_2d_A(x_0).
$$

Furthermore, when the matrix exponential norms satisfy $\|e^{At}\|,\|e^{A't}\|\le Me^{\alpha t}$, the time window during which the two trajectories remain $\epsilon$-close within $[0,T]$ can be controlled by the Lambert $W$ function. This result translates "near-invariant subspaces" into practical estimation difficulties: the smaller $d_A(x_0)$ and the smaller the difference between candidate matrices, the harder it is for finite-time observations to exclude incorrect systems.

### A Complete Example

One can think of the core phenomenon of the paper as a three-dimensional sparse system. In the true matrix $A$, only a few variables interact, and some columns provide almost no independent information outward due to the zero-pattern, placing $A$ near or even in the region of $\mathrm{rank}(A)\le n-2$. A researcher observes a complete trajectory starting from an initial condition $x_0$; both SINDy and Neural ODE can find a matrix $\hat{A}$ such that the trajectory integrated from the same $x_0$ almost coincides with the observed curve.

The problem is that coinciding trajectories only indicate that $A$ and $\hat{A}$ act similarly in the directions actually traversed by the trajectory. If $x_0$ is near an $A$-invariant proper subspace, the trajectory mainly stays near that subspace; another matrix $A'$ can generate almost identical observations in finite time as long as it matches $A$ on that subspace and differs on the complement space. In this case, downstream algorithms report high $R^2$ and low MSE, but the normalized Hamming distance remains large, indicating that the recovered non-zero edge structure is not the original system structure.

### Loss & Training

The paper does not train its own new models but designs three types of empirical validations. The first type directly calculates the rank, eigenvalues, and second smallest singular value $\sigma_2$ of real system matrices $A$ to test if system-level unidentifiability conditions show threshold behavior with sparsity. The second type looks only at trajectories, evaluating trajectory-level identifiability using the approximate $d_{A,0}(x_0)=\|x_0\|^{-1}\mathrm{dist}(x_0,\ker(A))$ and the smoothed condition number (SCN) from Qiu et al. The third type uses SINDy and sparse Neural ODEs to estimate system matrices, then compares the estimated sparsity patterns with the true patterns using normalized Hamming distance.

Data generation covers $n\in\{3,5,10,20,30,40,50\}$ and $p\in\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,0.9,0.95,1.0\}$. For each $(n,p)$, 100 sparse-continuous matrices are generated with non-zero weights from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$; for each matrix, 100 initial conditions on the unit sphere are sampled, and 512 time steps are generated on $[0,1]$ using RK45. To focus the conclusions on identifiability rather than optimization failure, only estimates with sufficiently good trajectory reconstruction are kept: typical thresholds are $R^2>0.99$ and MSE $<10^{-4}$. SINDy uses linear basis functions, L2 regularization, and sequential thresholded least squares; Neural ODE uses a linear network without activation functions and adds L1 weight regularization to encourage sparsity.

### Key Findings

- The most important finding of the system-level experiments is that the threshold behavior is very clear: as $p$ increases, rank deficiency, repeated eigenvalue conditions, and zero eigenvalue conditions rise almost simultaneously, aligning with the theoretical boundary $p_c(n)=1-\log(n)/n$.
- Trajectory-level experiments show that being "near rank deficient" at the system level translates to trajectory identifiability: systems with small $\sigma_2$ are not only dangerous at the matrix level but also generate trajectories with larger SCN and smaller distance metrics.
- A special phenomenon occurs in the highly sparse, low-dimensional region for SINDy: default sequential thresholding hard-zeros small coefficients, which may unexpectedly lower the Hamming distance in extremely sparse, low-dimensional cases; however, this bias advantage disappears as the dimension increases or sparsity returns to medium ranges.
- The appendix supplements the sparse model without zero rows/columns, illustrating that the problem addressed is not just the obvious source of "all-zero columns" but a broader identifiability gap caused by sparse structures.

## Highlights & Insights

- The greatest value of this paper is re-adding applicability conditions to a conclusion that is often oversimplified in the community. "Linear ODEs are almost always identifiable" is not wrong, but it assumes a dense continuous measure; once entering the sparse world, the zero-patterns themselves change the probability space.
- The authors do not stop at "constructing a counterexample" but provide positive probability lower bounds and asymptotic thresholds. This allows readers to roughly judge risks based on system dimension and sparsity rather than just receiving an abstract warning of unidentifiability.
- The treatment of near-unidentifiability is practical. Real system identification rarely needs to discuss mathematically identical trajectories; more often, it is about two systems being indistinguishable under finite time and precision. $d_A(x_0)$ and Lemma 4 connect this practical difficulty with the geometry of invariant subspaces.
- The experiments on SINDy and Neural ODE provide a direct reminder: the model's ability to reconstruct the observed trajectory only indicates it found a dynamics explaining the observations, not that it found the true mechanism. This insight is especially vital for scientific machine learning, where non-zero edges, intervention mechanisms, and variable relationships are of primary interest.

## Limitations & Future Work

- The theoretical analysis remains limited to linear, homogeneous, autonomous, fully observed, continuous, and noise-free ODEs. The authors emphasize this is to study intrinsic unidentifiability in a best-case scenario, but real systems involve discrete sampling, noise, external inputs, and partial observations.
- Only single trajectories are primarily discussed. If multiple initial conditions can be actively selected, or if the state space is better covered through interventions/control probing, the identifiability conclusions would change. Future systematic research is needed on "how many and what kind of trajectories" are needed to escape the sparse unidentifiability zone.
- While the sparse-continuous ensemble is a clear theoretical model, real network sparse patterns often have modules, hubs, hierarchies, and sign constraints. The appendix provides general sparsity-pattern lower bounds, but a complete calculable risk assessment tool for real-world network families is not yet formed.
- Empirical parts mainly look at linear SINDy and linearized Neural ODEs. For more complex neural dynamics, symbolic regression, or models with physical priors, theoretical unidentifiability should still exist, but practical performance and diagnostic metrics require further verification.
- The paper acknowledges it is difficult to judge if a learned structure is true from data alone. An important follow-up direction is designing reportable indices for uncertainty or equivalence classes, so that system identification methods do not output a seemingly certain matrix but rather report which edges, subspaces, or intervention conclusions are trustworthy.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs Stanhope et al. / Qiu et al. identifiability results for linear ODEs**: These works established the core conditions for identifying linear ODEs from single trajectories, proving the unidentifiable set is small in the dense case; Ours adopts their Jordan block and invariant subspace characterizations but points out that sparse distributions do not satisfy dense measure assumptions.
- **vs SINDy**: SINDy learns governing equations from trajectories and derivatives via sparse regression; Ours does not improve SINDy but shows that even if SINDy fits trajectories well, it may recover incorrect sparse structures in sparse unidentifiable regions.
- **vs Neural ODE**: Neural ODE focuses on parameterizing continuous-time dynamics with neural networks, usually for trajectory prediction; Ours warns that fitting success of Neural ODE should not be directly interpreted as identifying the true mechanism, especially when the network structure is sparse and the initial condition explores only a small part of the state space.
- **vs Identifiability work in non-linear dynamical systems**: Non-linear systems generally require stronger coverage or function class constraints for identification; Ours fills a gap in linear systems where "linear is safer than non-linear" was a common intuition that requires caution in sparse real systems.
- **Insights**: For scientific machine learning, future method evaluation should not just report trajectory reconstruction, but also structural recovery, the number of equivalent models, near-unidentifiability indices, and extrapolation performance under new initial or intervention conditions.

## Rating

- Novelty: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆ Correcting classical linear ODE identifiability conclusions from a sparse random matrix perspective; well-targeted problem and clear theoretical contribution.
- Experimental Thoroughness: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆ Theoretical validation, trajectory metrics, and practical estimators provide three layers of support; however, the real network section is more of a statistical verification than a full real-data system identification experiment.
- Writing Quality: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆ Clear main line, honest discussion of assumptions, and natural connection between theory and practice; some proofs and figures require a background in linear algebra and random graphs.
- Value: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ High warning value for work using ODE / Neural ODE / SINDy for scientific discovery, especially in correcting the common misinterpretation that "good fit equals correct mechanism."
