# X-MuTeST: A Multilingual Benchmark for Explainable Hate Speech Detection and A Novel LLM-consulted Explanation Framework

**Conference**: AAAI 2026
**arXiv**: [2601.03194](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03194)  
**Code**: [https://github.com/ziarehman30/X-MuTeST](https://github.com/ziarehman30/X-MuTeST)  
**Area**: NLP Understanding / Multilingual
**Keywords**: Hate Speech Detection, Explainability, Multilingual, LLM Explanation, Human-Annotated Rationales

## TL;DR
This paper proposes the X-MuTeST framework, which combines LLM semantic reasoning with a two-stage training strategy enhanced by n-gram attention for explainable multilingual hate speech detection. It also introduces the first token-level human-annotated rationale benchmark datasets for Hindi and Telugu.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Hate speech detection has evolved from pure classification toward providing explainable rationales. A small number of datasets with human-annotated rationales exist for English (e.g., HateXplain), but low-resource languages such as Hindi and Telugu lack such resources almost entirely.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Although LLMs can identify explicit hate terms (e.g., "dog"), they fall short in recognizing implicit hate expressions embedded in cultural contexts (e.g., "barking" as a slur in Hindi). A significant gap exists between machine-generated rationales and human rationales, particularly for low-resource languages. Additionally, traditional attention-based explanation methods lack semantic reasoning capability.

**Key Challenge**: A disconnect between classification accuracy and explanation fidelity — models may arrive at the correct label for the wrong reasons, especially in culturally situated low-resource language contexts.

**Goal**: (1) Provide token-level human-annotated rationale benchmarks for Hindi, Telugu, and English; (2) Design a hybrid explainability framework that integrates LLM explanations with the model's own attention signals.

**Key Insight**: Human rationales are used as training signals to align model attention with human judgment; n-gram perturbation then generates model-driven explanations; the final rationale is obtained by taking the union of model-derived and LLM-derived explanations.

**Core Idea**: A two-stage training procedure — Stage 1 uses human rationales to guide attention alignment; Stage 2 replaces human rationales with n-gram explainability scores derived from the model itself for further fine-tuning. The final explanation is the union of LLM and n-gram outputs, balancing semantic and syntactic coverage.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
Input text is encoded by a pretrained encoder (MuRIL or XLM-R). Training proceeds in two stages: Stage 1 uses human rationales to guide attention alignment (3 epochs); Stage 2 continues training using attention masks generated by the n-gram explainability method. The final explanation is obtained by taking the union of rationales from the X-MuTeST n-gram method and LLaMA-3.1.

### Key Designs

1. **Human Rationale-Guided Attention Alignment (Stage 1)**:

    - **Function**: Align the tokens attended to by the model with human judgment.
    - **Mechanism**: The attention score for each token is computed as $a_i = \text{softmax}(h_i^\top h_{[CLS]})$. An attention alignment loss $\mathcal{L}_{att} = -\sum_i R_i \log a_i$ guides the model to focus on human-annotated rationale tokens. The total loss is $\mathcal{L}_1 = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{att} + (1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{cl}$, with $\alpha=0.3$.
    - **Design Motivation**: Directly training model attention on rationale tokens bridges the gap between machine-generated and human rationales.

2. **N-gram Perturbation Explainability Method (Stage 2)**:

    - **Function**: Generate model-driven token importance scores.
    - **Mechanism**: Each unigram, bigram, and trigram of the input text is individually fed to the model, and the logit drop relative to the original prediction probability is computed. Token-level importance is then aggregated across all n-grams containing that token: $E[t] = \sum_{n=1}^{3} w_n \cdot \frac{1}{N_t^{(n)}} \sum_{ng \ni t} \Delta P_{ng}$, with weights 0.5/0.3/0.2. The top-$k$ tokens are selected as attention targets for Stage 2.
    - **Design Motivation**: Human rationales provide an initial guide, but the model's own learned features may capture more contextually relevant tokens. Alternating between the two stages balances human knowledge and model insight.

3. **LLM-consulted Hybrid Explanation**:

    - **Function**: Produce the final explainable rationale.
    - **Mechanism**: The final explanation is $\mathcal{E}_{final} = \mathcal{E}_X \cup \mathcal{E}_{LLM}$, where $\mathcal{E}_X$ comprises the top tokens from the n-gram method and $\mathcal{E}_{LLM}$ comprises hate-indicative tokens identified by LLaMA-3.1. The union ensures dual coverage at both syntactic and semantic levels.
    - **Design Motivation**: LLMs excel at semantic reasoning but lack cultural sensitivity, while the n-gram method captures task-specific salient tokens but lacks semantic understanding; the two are complementary.

### Loss & Training
Both stages share the same loss structure: $\mathcal{L} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{att} + (1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{cl}$. In Stage 1, $\alpha=0.3$; in Stage 2, $\alpha$ varies by language (0.6 for Telugu and English, 0.7 for Hindi). Encoder selection: MuRIL for Telugu and English, XLM-R for Hindi.

### Key Findings
- Human rationale-guided training significantly improves both classification and explainability performance (Acc: 0.860 → 0.874).
- The X-MuTeST method outperforms LIME across all evaluation metrics.
- LLMs (GPT-4o, LLaMA-3.1) achieve substantially lower classification accuracy than fine-tuned models, yet LLaMA-3.1 produces higher-quality rationales (Token-F1 = 0.515) than GPT-4o.
- An iterative annotation protocol improves inter-annotator agreement from moderate to high levels (Kappa 81–85).

## Highlights & Insights
- **Low-Resource Language Rationale Datasets**: This work is the first to provide token-level hate rationale annotations for Hindi and Telugu, filling a critical data gap in cross-lingual explainable hate detection. The value of such resources extends well beyond this single paper.
- **Elegant Two-Stage Training Design**: Stage 1 uses human rationales to "warm-start" attention direction; Stage 2 fine-tunes using model-discovered important tokens, avoiding both the bias of relying solely on human rationales and the unreliability of relying solely on model-derived ones.
- **LLM as Explanation Auxiliary, Not Primary**: The paper recognizes that LLMs underperform fine-tuned models in classification but offer complementary semantic reasoning that can be leveraged for explanation.

## Limitations & Future Work
- The dataset scale is limited (approximately 17K samples across three languages), making it difficult to cover all forms of hate speech expression.
- The choice of LLM (LLaMA-3.1) is based on empirical comparison rather than systematic evaluation.
- Only token-level explanations are addressed; sentence-level or discourse-level rationale reasoning is not explored.
- The framework can be extended to additional low-resource languages, such as Bengali, Urdu, and other South Asian languages.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. HateXplain (Mathew et al., 2021)**: The first English hate rationale dataset; this work extends it to a multilingual setting and proposes a stronger training framework.
- **vs. LIME/SHAP**: Traditional post-hoc explanation methods; the proposed n-gram method is more direct and optimized for NLP tasks.
- The two-stage "human → model" rationale transition strategy is transferable to other NLP tasks requiring explainability.

## Rating
- Novelty: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The multilingual rationale dataset and the framework combining two-stage training with LLM-consulted explanation are notably novel.
- Experimental Thoroughness: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Evaluation across three languages with multiple baselines, including LLM baselines.
- Writing Quality: ⭐⭐⭐ The structure is complete, but some descriptions are verbose and mathematical notation could be more concise.
- Value: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The dataset contribution has lasting value for low-resource language research.
