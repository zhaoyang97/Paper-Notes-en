# INJONGO: A Multicultural Intent Detection and Slot-filling Dataset for 16 African Languages

**Conference**: ACL 2025  
**arXiv**: [2502.09814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09814)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: Others  
**Keywords**: Low-resource languages, intent detection, slot filling, African languages, multicultural dataset  

## TL;DR

This paper introduces Injongo, a multicultural intent detection and slot-filling benchmark dataset covering 16 African languages. It is organically generated by native speakers across domains such as banking, travel, home, and food/beverage. Experiments reveal that LLMs perform extremely poorly on slot filling in African languages (GPT-4o achieves only 26 F1), and intent detection also lags significantly behind fine-tuned baselines.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Intent detection and slot filling are foundational tasks for dialog AI. Although existing large-scale benchmarks (such as MASSIVE, MultiATIS++) cover dozens of languages, their support for low-resource African languages is extremely weak. More importantly, almost all corpora in these benchmarks are translated from English, reflecting Western cultural scenarios.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Translated datasets have two fundamental issues: (1) Cultural bias—the actual expressions and concerns of African users in banking, dining, and other scenarios differ significantly from those of Western users (e.g., "money transfer" in Africa might involve mobile payments like M-Pesa rather than bank cards); (2) Deficit in language coverage—most African languages are completely absent from existing benchmarks, making model capabilities on these languages entirely unknown.

**Key Challenge**: If only Western-centric translated data is used to evaluate and train models, their performance in real-world scenarios for African users will be systematically overestimated. There is a need to reconstruct datasets from cultural roots by native speakers.

**Goal**: (1) Construct a multicultural benchmark organically created by native speakers; (2) Systematically evaluate the performance of multilingual Transformer fine-tuning and LLM prompting on African languages; (3) Compare the effectiveness of African cultural corpora versus Western-centric corpora in cross-lingual transfer.

**Key Insight**: The authors organized a team of native researchers from various African countries to directly write natural corpora in their target languages within four everyday domains, ensuring the content reflects local cultural scenarios and expression habits.

**Core Idea**: Build a multilingual benchmark through organic generation rather than translation, covering four daily domains across 16 African languages. It provides intent annotations and BIO slot annotations to evaluate the capabilities of current models in truly low-resource, culturally specific scenarios.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

The construction and evaluation of the Injongo dataset comprise three stages: (1) Data collection: native speakers generate natural utterances in four domains (banking, travel, home, and food/beverage) and annotate intents and slots; (2) Fine-tuning evaluation: multilingual Transformers such as mBERT and XLM-R are fine-tuned on English data and then cross-lingually transferred to African languages; (3) LLM prompt evaluation: large models such as GPT-4o are evaluated directly under zero-shot and few-shot settings for intent detection and slot filling.

### Key Designs

1. **Native-speaker-originated multicultural corpus collection**:

    - **Function**: Construct culturally appropriate and naturally spoken dialog data.
    - **Mechanism**: Recruit native annotators for 16 languages (covering multiple language families such as Bantu, Nilotic, Afroasiatic, and Niger-Congo), with 2-3 annotators per language. Annotators first receive training on intent/slot annotation guidelines, and then freely create utterances within given domains. Crucially, no English templates are provided—annotators think and express directly in their native languages. Each domain defines several intent types (e.g., banking: transfer money, check balance, open account, etc.) and slot types (e.g., amount, recipient, account type, etc.), but culturally specific intents and slots are allowed to emerge.
    - **Design Motivation**: Translation not only introduces "translationese" but, more importantly, restricts the scope of the corpus content. If "M-Pesa transfer" is absent from the original English template, translated versions will never capture this intent.

2. **Cross-lingual transfer evaluation framework**:

    - **Function**: Evaluate the effectiveness of knowledge transfer from high-resource languages to low-resource African languages.
    - **Mechanism**: The training phase consists of two settings: (1) fine-tuning XLM-R on standard English data (the MASSIVE English subset); (2) fine-tuning on back-translated English data from Injongo. In inference, models are tested directly on the 16 African languages. By comparing the performance differences between the two training datasets, the study quantifies the value of "culturally appropriate training data" for cross-lingual transfer.
    - **Design Motivation**: This comparison directly addresses a crucial question: is cross-lingual transfer more effective when utilizing English training data based on Western scenarios, or English training data situated in African cultural contexts?

3. **LLM zero-shot/few-shot evaluation**:

    - **Function**: Evaluate the zero-shot capability of state-of-the-art LLMs on low-resource African languages.
    - **Mechanism**: Evaluate GPT-4o under zero-shot (direct task description) and few-shot (with 5-shot exemplars) settings. Accuracy is used for intent detection, and F1 score is used for slot filling.
    - **Design Motivation**: LLMs are widely regarded as "general-purpose" models, yet their actual capabilities on low-resource languages lack systematic evaluation. Injongo provides a testing ground under real-world scenarios.

### Loss & Training

The standard cross-entropy loss is used during the fine-tuning phase. Intent detection is framed as a multi-class classification task, while slot filling is treated as a BIO sequence tagging task. XLM-R utilizes adapter-based fine-tuning to reduce parameter footprint.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

| Method | Intent Detection (Acc %) | Slot Filling (F1) | Description |
|------|-----------------|--------------|------|
| XLM-R Fine-tuned (MASSIVE English) | 68.3 | 42.1 | Western-centric training data |
| XLM-R Fine-tuned (Injongo English) | 73.5 | 48.7 | African-cultural training data, +5.2% |
| GPT-4o Zero-shot | 70.6 | 26.0 | Intent detection is decent, slot filling is extremely poor |
| GPT-4o Few-shot | 74.2 | 31.5 | Few-shot shows improvement but still lags far behind fine-tuning |
| GPT-4o (English only) | 81.0 | 65.3 | Performs significantly better on English |

### Ablation Study

| Language/Family | Intent Detection (XLM-R) | Slot Filling (XLM-R) | Description |
|----------|-----------------|-----------------|------|
| Swahili (Bantu) | 78.1 | 55.3 | Best performance, relatively more training data |
| Hausa (Afroasiatic) | 72.4 | 47.6 | Upper-middle performance |
| Yoruba (Niger-Congo) | 69.8 | 43.2 | Moderate performance, complex morphology |
| Amharic (Semitic) | 65.2 | 38.1 | Non-Latin script increases difficulty |
| Wolof (Atlantic) | 58.7 | 29.4 | Extremely scarce data, worst performance |

### Key Findings

- **GPT-4o performs extremely poorly on slot filling** (averaging only 26 F1), showing that while LLMs possess some multilingual understanding capabilities, they are entirely inadequate for fine-grained sequence labeling in low-resource languages.
- **Models trained on African cultural corpora outperform those trained on Western-centric corpora** (+5.2% in intent detection), directly demonstrating the value of culturally appropriate training data.
- **Resource availability and writing systems are two key factors**—languages using the Latin alphabet with more pre-training data (e.g., Swahili) demonstrate the best performance.
- **The difficulty gap between intent detection and slot filling is immense**—as intent detection is a sentence-level classification task, GPT-4o manages it through cross-lingual comprehension; in contrast, slot filling requires precise token-level alignment, which is far beyond the current low-resource capabilities of modern LLMs.

## Highlights & Insights

- **The "organic vs. translated" comparative experimental design** directly quantifies the impact of cultural bias on model evaluation. This serves as a cautionary tale for all multilingual NLP research: benchmarks built solely through translation may systematically overestimate model performance on low-resource languages.
- **The massive performance gap of GPT-4o between English and African languages** (81% vs. 70.6% for intent detection, 65.3 vs. 26.0 for slot filling) clearly quantifies the current LLM deficit in "language equity".
- **The dataset construction methodology** can be generalized to other low-resource language communities—Southeast Asian and South Asian languages, for instance, face similar translation bias issues.

## Limitations & Future Work

- The data volume per language remains small (due to high annotation costs), which may be insufficient to support in-language fine-tuning.
- Only 4 domains are covered, while practical dialogue AI scenarios are far more complex.
- Evaluated LLMs are limited (primarily GPT-4o); the capabilities of open-source models (such as Llama, Mistral) on African languages remain untested.
- Strategies such as data augmentation or adversarial training were not explored to improve performance on low-resource languages.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs. MASSIVE (FitzGerald et al. 2022)**: MASSIVE covers 51 languages but is entirely translated from English. Injongo avoids translation bias by utilizing organic generation; though narrower in coverage, it offers higher cultural authenticity.
- **vs. MultiATIS++ (Xu et al. 2020)**: MultiATIS++ is also translation-based and does not include African languages. Injongo fills this gap.
- **vs. AfriQA (Ogundepo et al. 2023)**: AfriQA focuses on QA tasks in African languages, while Injongo addresses dialogue understanding tasks, rendering the two complementary.

## Rating

- **Novelty**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The dataset construction methodology (organic generation by native speakers + cultural comparison) is valuable, though the room for innovation in benchmark-oriented work is inherently limited.
- **Experimental Thoroughness**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The comparison between fine-tuning and zero-shot/few-shot LLMs is comprehensive, but more models could have been evaluated.
- **Writing Quality**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Detailed dataset description, with insightful analyses of cultural discrepancies.
- **Value**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Significant contribution to the low-resource NLP community, driving progress in AI research for African languages.
