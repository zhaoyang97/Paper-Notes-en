# Why Less is More (Sometimes): A Theory of Data Curation

**Conference**: ICLR 2026  
**Paper**: [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=why_less_is_more_sometimes_a_theory_of_data_curation) (⚠️ Link based on original)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: Learning Theory / Data Curation / High-dimensional Statistics  
**Keywords**: Data Curation, Scaling Laws, Phase Transition Analysis, Model Collapse, Random Matrix Theory

## TL;DR
This paper utilizes high-dimensional binary classification and Random Matrix Theory (RMT) to derive **precise test error scaling curves** for data curation strategies such as "keep hard samples," "keep easy samples," and "full training." It proves that the "less is more" phenomenon (where a small subset of high-quality data is superior) only occurs in the quadrant of "abundant data + sufficiently strong generator," providing a unified theoretical explanation for why methods like LIMO and s1 are effective yet fail on the most difficult tasks.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Classical scaling laws (Kaplan, Hoffmann, etc.) suggest that "more is more"—more data leads to better generalization, leading to the training of foundational models on hundreds of billions of tokens. However, during training, all samples are treated equally in the loss function, regardless of their informativeness.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Recent works (LIMO = Less Is More, s1) have shown the opposite: selecting a small subset of "correct and difficult" samples can enhance reasoning performance, where a few thousand samples outperformed hundreds of thousands. This directly conflicts with "more is more," but prior evidence remains empirical without a theoretical explanation of **when to prefer less and when to prefer more**. Worse, contradictory phenomena appear in the same dataset: on AIME average scores, "less is more" wins, but for the most difficult problems, "more is more" returns.

**Key Challenge**: Whether data curation is harmful or beneficial depends on a set of entangled factors: **the accuracy of the "generator" producing training labels, the accuracy of the "oracle" performing the selection, the alignment between the two, and the total data volume**. Without a precise characterization of these quantities, the "less vs. more" debate remains heuristic.

**Goal**: Instead of proposing another heuristic curation trick, this work aims to establish a theoretical framework capable of **analytically solving the test error**, transforming the decision of "when to prune and when to scale" into calculable phase transition conditions.

**Key Insight**: Compress the problem into a minimal model that is both rich and analytically tractable—**high-dimensional isotropic binary classification** with a pruning oracle based on "difficulty + correctness." Use RMT to solve for the closed-form test error in the proportional limit where $n,d\to\infty,\ d/n\to\phi$.

**Core Idea**: Utilize three geometric alignment metrics $(\rho,\rho^*,\rho_g)$ to characterize "generator quality / oracle quality / alignment between them." This proves that the optimal curation strategy undergoes a **phase transition** based on the generator quality $\rho$: strong generators should "keep hard samples," while weak generators should "keep easy samples."

## Method

This paper is a purely theoretical/analytical work without a trainable pipeline. The core consists of mathematical modeling and three theorems. Thus, instead of an architecture diagram, the chain of "modeling-metrics-theorems" is described via text and formulas.

### Overall Architecture

A **teacher-student high-dimensional classification** problem is defined. Data is generated by a distribution $P_g=P_{w_g,C_g}$: $x\sim N(0,C_g)$ with labels $y=\mathrm{sign}(x^\top w_g)$. The true test distribution is $P_*=P_{w_*,\Sigma}$. Generally, $w_g\neq w_*$ (label shift) and $C_g\neq\Sigma$ (covariate shift). The main results focus on the isotropic case with only label shift ($C_g=\Sigma=I_d$).

The learner solves a ridge regression problem (squared loss) with a sample mask:

$$\min_{w}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\,\ell(x_i^\top w; y_i)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2,\quad \ell(z;y)=\tfrac12(z-y)^2,$$

where $p_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether sample $i$ is retained. The optimal solution has a closed form $\hat w=RX^\top DY/n$, where $R=(S+\lambda I_d)^{-1}$, $S=X^\top DX/n$, and $D=\mathrm{diag}(p_i)$. The downstream classifier is $x\mapsto\mathrm{sign}(x^\top\hat w)$, and the object of study is the test error $E_{\text{test}}(\hat w)=P(\mathrm{sign}(x^\top\hat w)\neq y)$.

Constraint: The learner **cannot see** the oracle direction $w_o$, nor the oracle labels $y_o$. It can only submit samples $(x_i,y_i)$ and receive a bit $p_i$. The theory identifies which samples to pick and how many to minimize $E_{\text{test}}$ under this "black-box curation."

### Key Designs

**1. Unifying Data Curation into Two Types of Pruning Oracles**
Prior curation rules are diverse (margin difficulty, label correctness, LIMO-style "difficult and diverse"). This work unifies them using an oracle direction $w_o$ and a symmetric function $q$:

- **Label-agnostic**: Based only on features, $p_i=q(x_i^\top w_o)$. Setting $q(t)=\mathbf 1[|t|\ge\alpha]$ defines **Keep Easy (KE)** (far from decision boundary, large margin), while $q(t)=\mathbf 1[|t|\le\alpha]$ defines **Keep Hard (KH)** (near boundary). The threshold $\alpha$ controls the retention ratio.
- **Label-aware**: Close to LIMO/s1, where samples are kept if the "oracle deems the label correct" and the sample is "interesting": $p_i=1\iff y_i=y_i^o\ \text{and}\ q(x_i^\top w_o)=1$, where $y_i^o=\mathrm{sign}(x_i^\top w_o)$.

The retention ratio $p:=P(p_i=1)$ is the pruning ratio. $p \to 1$ implies the full dataset. This modeling allows all conclusions to enter the error formula via several scalars, enabling analytical comparison.

**2. Three Metrics to Quantify Quality**
This is the core of the work. The "less vs. more" debate is essentially a comparison of weights. The paper defines three cosines and one cotangent via Mahalanobis inner products:

$$\rho=\frac{w_g^\top C w_*}{\|w_g\|_C\|w_*\|_C},\quad \rho^*=\frac{w_o^\top C w_*}{\|w_o\|_C\|w_*\|_C},\quad \rho_g=\frac{w_o^\top C w_g}{\|w_o\|_C\|w_g\|_C},\quad \tau=\frac{\rho_g}{\sqrt{1-\rho_g^2}}.$$

Here, $\rho$=**generator quality** (cosine between training labeler $w_g$ and truth $w_*$), $\rho^*$=**oracle quality**, and $\rho_g$=alignment between oracle and generator. These determine baseline errors: $E_{\text{test}}(w_g)=\frac1\pi\arccos\rho$, $E_{\text{test}}(w_o)=\frac1\pi\arccos\rho^*$. $\rho\to1$ denotes a strong generator. Phase transitions depend on $(\rho,\rho_g,\rho^*)$.

**3. Precise Test Error via RMT (Theorem 1)**
In high dimensions, $\hat w$ is a function of a random matrix. The paper constructs deterministic equivalents for the resolvent $R$ and $R^2$, compressing the impact of any strategy $q$ into four scalars:

$$p=\mathbb E[q(G)],\ \gamma=\mathbb E[q(G)G^2],\ \beta=2\mathbb E[q(G)\varphi(\tau G)],\ \tilde\beta=2\mathbb E[q(G)\Phi(\tau G)G],$$

where $G\sim N(0,1)$, and $\varphi,\Phi$ are standard normal PDF/CDF. The test error converges in the proportional limit to:

$$E_{\text{test}}(\hat w)\to\frac1\pi\arccos\!\big(|m_0|/\sqrt{\nu_0}\big),$$

where $m_0,\nu_0$ are given by the scalars, $\lambda$, and the alignment trio. Substituting the four scalars for a strategy yields the error curve.

**4. Phase Transition of Optimal Curation (Theorem 2)**
In the limit of abundant data and zero regularization, the error functional $F(q)$ is defined. For a fixed $p$ and $\rho_g>0$:

- **(A) Strong Generator ($\rho\to1$) + Strong Oracle ($\rho^*\to1$)**: The unique optimum is **Keep Hard (KH)**. Intuition: The model has learned the basics; simple samples are redundant. Difficult samples provide "refinement"—the basis for LIMO's "less is more."
- **(B) Weak Generator ($\rho<1$) + Strong Oracle ($\rho^*\to1$)**: The optimum flips to **Keep Easy (KE)**. Intuition: The weak model lacks a foundation; difficult samples are too noisy. Clean, easy samples are needed to build the base. This helps alleviate **model collapse** during self-training.

**Core Idea**: The optimal strategy is not universal; it undergoes a phase transition between KH and KE based on generator strength relative to the task.

### A Concrete Example

Consider LLM mathematical reasoning. If a base LLM (generating $w_g$) is a strong generator for **most AIME problems** (high $\rho$): this falls under Theorem 2(A). Radical pruning (KH) is optimal. LIMO/s1 with 1k selected samples (56.7%) outperforms 114k (50.2%).

However, for the **most difficult AIME problems**: the same LLM becomes a weak generator (low $\rho$) relative to these problems. This falls into the "more is more" side, where performance increases monotonically with data: 1k (28.4%) to 1M (64.9%). The same model and pool exhibit a "paradox" explained by the flipping of $\rho$ due to task difficulty.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

Synthetic experiments validated the theory on a 2×2 grid: generator quality ($\rho=1$ vs. $\rho<1$) and data volume (small $n=100$ vs. large $n=5000$). Empirical results matched theoretical curves perfectly. **"Less is more" only holds in the [Abundant Data + Strong Generator] quadrant.**

| Data/Generator Quadrant | Optimal Pruning Ratio $p$ | Conclusion |
|------|------|------|
| Low Data + Strong Generator | $p=1$ | more is more |
| Low Data + Weak Generator | $p=1$ | more is more |
| High Data + Weak Generator | $p=1$ | more is more |
| **High Data + Strong Generator** | $p\ll1$ (Aggressive Pruning) | **less is more** |

Real-world AIME reasoning tables confirm the transition:

| Setting | Training Data Size | Metric | Performance | Principle |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| AIME Average (Strong Gen) | 0 / 114k / 59k / **1k** | Pass@1 | 16.5 / 50.2 / 53.3 / **56.7** | less is more |
| AIME Hardest (Weak Gen) | 0 / 1k / 2k / 10k / **1M** | Avg@8 | 1.0 / 28.4 / 35.4 / 52.1 / **64.9** | more is more |

### Ablation Study

Experiments on ImageNet scaled the initial training set size $n$ to adjust generator strength, observing the KE/KH crossover:

| Configuration | Phenomenon | Explanation |
|------|------|------|
| Low Data (160K, Weak Gen) | Keep Easy is better | Confirms Theorem 2(B) |
| High Data (1.2M, Strong Gen) | Keep Hard is better | Confirms Theorem 2(A) |
| Iterative Self-Training + Full | Performance degrades (Collapse) | Full training is incorrect |
| Iterative Self-Training + KH | Stable performance | Curation saves the model |

### Key Findings
- **Phase transition points exist**: On ImageNet, as $n$ increases, the optimal strategy shifts smoothly from KE to KH, driven by $\rho$.
- **Curation prevents model collapse**: In pseudo-labeling loops, full training leads to decay, while keeping "hard and correct" samples stabilizes performance.
- **A model can be both a strong/weak generator**: It depends on the task difficulty slice, explaining conflicting "less/more" conclusions on the same benchmark.

## Highlights & Insights
- **Quantifying Hegemony**: Transforms the "less vs. more" debate into a calculable Theorem 2 condition using three cosines $(\rho,\rho^*,\rho_g)$.
- **Precise Closed-form Solutions**: RMT provides exact errors rather than bounds, allowing for quantitative optimization of "how much to prune."
- **Counter-intuitive Insight**: Weak models should be conservative (KE), while strong models should be aggressive (KH). This guides RLHF and self-distillation curricula.
- **Unified Paradox Explanation**: Explains why LIMO works on average but fails on hard subsets by identifying how difficulty flips the $\rho$ value.

## Limitations & Future Work
- **Minimalistic Model**: Results are based on high-dimensional **linear binary classification** + squared loss. Extrapolation to nonlinear, sequential LLMs with cross-entropy requires caution (currently based on phenomenal consistency).
- **Label Shift focus**: Main text assumes $C_g=\Sigma=I_d$, moving covariate shift to the appendix.
- **Oracle Quality Control**: Assumes $\rho^*$ is a known knob, whereas estimating pruner accuracy in reality is difficult.
- **Future Directions**: Extending theory to multi-class/nonlinear features and estimating $\rho/\rho^*$ proxies for real-world LLMs.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. Sorscher et al. (2022)**: They empirically showed margin pruning "bends" scaling laws; this work provides the **exact analytical conditions** for when that bending occurs.
- **vs. LIMO / s1**: These are empirical; this work provides the theoretical foundation and identifies the failure zones (weak generators).
- **vs. Feng et al. / Firdoussi et al.**: Their "correctness only" oracles are special cases ($q\equiv1$) of the generalized oracle proposed here.
- **vs. Model Collapse Theories**: While previous work emphasized irreversibility, this work proves **strategic curation can actively prevent collapse**.
