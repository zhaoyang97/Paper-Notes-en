# The Challenge of Teaching Reasoning to LLMs Without RL or Distillation

**Conference**: ICML 2025 (AI for Math Workshop)  
**arXiv**: [2507.09850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09850)  
**Code**: None (Dataset released: nvidia/Nemotron-Math-HumanReasoning)  
**Area**: Reinforcement Learning  
**Keywords**: Chain-of-Thought, Reasoning Ability, Fine-tuning, Long CoT, Human Annotation

## TL;DR

Lightweight fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-32B using only 20 long CoT examples from the reasoning model QwQ-32B-Preview outperforms a 72B math instruction model. However, CoTs generated by non-reasoning models or humans fail to achieve comparable effects, indicating a hard-to-replicate "latent quality" inherent in reasoning CoTs.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Language models with reasoning capabilities (such as o1 and DeepSeek-R1) achieve SOTA performance by generating long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) trajectories to solve complex tasks. Existing methods primarily obtain reasoning capabilities through two pathways: (1) RL training (such as GRPO, PPO), and (2) distillation from strong models.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Both RL training and distillation incur high costs—RL requires substantial sampling and training compute, while distillation requires access to powerful reasoning models. A natural question arises: can we activate the reasoning capabilities of base models through a simpler manner—using only a small number of CoT examples or purely human annotations?

**Key Challenge**: Prior work has shown that even short CoT prompting (without fine-tuning) can improve reasoning. Why then is RL or distillation still necessary? Can a small amount of high-quality CoT data replace large-scale RL/distillation? If not, what makes the CoTs generated by reasoning models so "special" that humans cannot replicate them?

**Goal**: Explore the boundaries of activating reasoning capabilities in the simplest way (minimal CoT + lightweight fine-tuning)—comparing reasoning model CoTs vs. non-reasoning model CoTs vs. human-authored CoTs to identify which can activate reasoning and locate the gap.

**Key Insight**: Conducting a systematic empirical study—controlling variables to compare the fine-tuning effects of different CoT sources (reasoning models, non-reasoning models, and humans) and analyzing critical attributes such as difficulty, diversity, and length.

**Core Idea**: Merely 20 long CoT examples generated by reasoning models are sufficient to unlock strong reasoning capabilities in base models. However, this effect stems from a hard-to-replicate "latent quality" in reasoning CoTs that non-reasoning models and human-annotated CoTs fail to match.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

This work does not propose a new algorithm but rather conducts a systematic empirical study. The core pipeline consists of:

1. **Collect CoT data from different sources**: Obtain long CoTs from QwQ-32B-Preview (reasoning model), non-reasoning models, and human annotators.
2. **Conduct lightweight fine-tuning on Qwen2.5-32B (base model)**: Use an extremely small number (as low as 20) of CoT examples.
3. **Evaluate on mathematical reasoning benchmarks**: Analyze the impact of different CoT sources on the final reasoning ability.
4. **Attribute analysis**: Investigate the impact of dimensions such as problem difficulty, diversity, and answer length on the performance.

### Key Designs

1. **Reasoning Model CoT Fine-Tuning (Reasoning Model CoT)**:

    - **Function**: Collect 20 long CoT examples from QwQ-32B-Preview to fine-tune the Qwen2.5-32B base model.
    - **Mechanism**: These CoTs contain deep thinking, backtracking, and verification processes, which are typically long (often thousands of tokens).
    - **Design Motivation**: Verify the hypothesis of whether "a small amount of high-quality reasoning data can replace large-scale training." The results indicate that 20 such examples are sufficient for a 32B base model to outperform a 72B mathematical instruction model.

2. **Non-Reasoning Model CoT**:

    - **Function**: Conduct the same fine-tuning experiments using CoTs generated by non-reasoning models (e.g., standard instruct models).
    - **Core Result**: The effects are significantly weaker than those of reasoning model CoTs, and this gap cannot be closed even by increasing the dataset size or optimizing prompts.
    - **Design Motivation**: Exclude the possibility that "any long CoT is effective" and prove the uniqueness of reasoning model CoTs.

3. **Human-Authored CoT**:

    - **Function**: Employ human annotators to write long CoTs and enhance their quality through prompt engineering, multi-pass editing, and structural guidance.
    - **Core Result**: Even after multiple rounds of meticulous editing, the fine-tuning effect of human CoTs still falls short of reasoning model CoTs.
    - **Design Motivation**: Explore whether human annotation can serve as an alternative source for reasoning data and understand the elements in reasoning CoTs that are difficult for humans to mimic.

4. **CoT Attribute Analysis**:

    - **Function**: Systematically analyze key data attributes affecting fine-tuning results.
    - **Key Findings**:
        - **Problem Difficulty**: Problems of moderate difficulty are more effective than those that are too easy or too hard.
        - **Diversity**: Problems from diverse mathematical subdomains outperform homogeneous ones.
        - **Answer Length**: Longer CoTs generally perform better, but there is a diminishing return.
    - **Design Motivation**: Provide a quantitative analytical framework to understand "what makes good reasoning data."

### Loss & Training

- Base Model: Qwen2.5-32B (base, non-instruct version)
- Fine-tuning Method: Standard SFT with extremely small data volume (20 examples are sufficient)
- Data Source Comparison: QwQ-32B-Preview / Standard instruct models / Human annotations
- Evaluation: Mathematical reasoning benchmarks such as MATH, GSM8K, and AIME

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

| CoT Source | Data Volume | MATH | Comparison |
|----------|-------|------|------|
| QwQ-32B-Preview (Reasoning Model) | 20 examples | Outperforms Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct | Baseline |
| Non-Reasoning Model | 20 examples | Significantly weaker than reasoning model CoT | Obvious gap |
| Human-Authored (Initial Version) | 20 examples | Weaker than reasoning model CoT | Underperforms |
| Human-Authored (Multi-pass Edited) | 20 examples | Improved but still inferior to reasoning model | Gap narrowed but not eliminated |
| Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct | - | Baseline | Outperformed by 32B fine-tuned on 20 reasoning CoTs |

### Ablation Study

| Attribute Change | Impact on Performance | Description |
|---------|---------|------|
| Increase CoT data volume | Diminishing returns | Decreased marginal gain beyond 20 examples |
| Increase problem difficulty | Moderate is best | Too easy yields no learning; too hard prevents learning |
| Increase domain diversity | Positive | Helpful to cover more mathematical subdomains |
| Increase CoT length | Positive with upper limit | Longer reasoning processes are generally better |
| Multi-pass human editing | Limited improvement | Narrows but cannot eliminate the gap with reasoning model CoT |
| Structural guidance | Limited improvement | Format optimization provides limited help |

### Key Findings

1. **Surprising Effect of 20 Examples**: Fine-tuning a 32B base model with only 20 long CoT examples from a reasoning model can outperform a specialized 72B math model. This suggests that reasoning capabilities may already lie "dormant" within base models and only require a small amount of high-quality data to activate.
2. **Irreplaceability of Reasoning CoT**: CoTs from non-reasoning models and human authors fail to match the effectiveness of reasoning model CoTs, even when the data volume is larger or carefully edited.
3. **"Latent Quality" in CoT**: Reasoning model-generated CoTs contain certain quality features that are difficult to reproduce manually—possibly representing implicit cognitive patterns, error correction strategies, or specific reasoning tempos.

## Highlights & Insights

- **Subversive Discovery**: Discovering that just 20 examples can activate strong reasoning capabilities challenges the mainstream view that "reasoning requires large-scale RL training."
- **Deeper Question**: What "magic" exists in reasoning CoTs that human annotators cannot replicate? This question points toward a deeper understanding of LLM reasoning mechanisms.
- **Practical Value**: Even if the final conclusion is "reasoning model CoTs are still needed," the efficiency of 20-shot fine-tuning is extremely high.
- **Dataset Contribution**: Releasing a multi-pass refined human CoT dataset (Nemotron-Math-HumanReasoning), providing a valuable resource for follow-up research.

## Limitations & Future Work

1. Validated only on mathematical reasoning tasks; conclusions may differ for other reasoning types (code, logical reasoning).
2. Lack of in-depth mechanistic explanations for the "latent quality in reasoning CoT"—remaining at a phenomenological level.
3. The quality of human-annotated CoTs is limited by the annotators' mathematical proficiency; annotations from professional mathematicians might align closer with reasoning models.
4. The sensitivity of the fine-tuning performance to the selection of the specific 20 examples has not been fully explored.
5. As a workshop paper, the scale and depth of the experiments are limited compared to main conference papers.

## Related Work & Insights

- **DeepSeek-R1-Zero/R1**: Representative works that acquire reasoning capabilities through RL or RL + distillation.
- **s1 (Simple Test-Time Scaling)**: A similar direction exploring the activation of reasoning capabilities with minimal data.
- **STaR**: Self-taught reasoning, iteratively improving reasoning via self-generated training data.
- **Insight**: Reasoning capabilities might be an "emergent" potential of base models, which can be awakened with minimal and correct stimulation—presenting new questions for understanding the internal reasoning mechanisms of LLMs.
