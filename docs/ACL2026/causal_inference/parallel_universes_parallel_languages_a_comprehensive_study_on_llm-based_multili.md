# Parallel Universes, Parallel Languages: A Comprehensive Study on LLM-based Multilingual Counterfactual Example Generation

**Conference**: ACL 2026  
**arXiv**: [2601.00263](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00263)  
**Code**: [GitHub](https://github.com/qiaw99/multicfe)  
**Area**: Causal Inference  
**Keywords**: Multilingual counterfactual generation, counterfactual explanation, data augmentation, cross-lingual consistency, LLM multilingual capability

## TL;DR

This paper systematically investigates the multilingual counterfactual example generation capabilities of LLMs across six languages. By comparing direct generation and translation paths, it finds that the translation path achieves a higher label flip rate but requires more edits. It identifies four common error patterns and verifies that multilingual counterfactual data augmentation outperforms cross-lingual augmentation, particularly for low-resource languages.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Counterfactual examples refer to instances where the input is minimally edited to change the model's prediction, serving as an effective means to explain model behavior. Existing counterfactual generation methods (such as MICE, Polyjuice, ZeroCF, etc.) are almost exclusively evaluated on English data.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: While LLMs have demonstrated strong multilingual capabilities, their effectiveness in generating high-quality counterfactuals in non-English languages remains unclear. Cross-lingual analysis has revealed systematic behavioral differences between English and non-English instances, suggesting that English counterfactuals alone are insufficient to capture the full picture of model behavior.

**Key Challenge**: The relationship between the multilingual capabilities of LLMs and their counterfactual generation ability has not been systematically studied—how large is the gap in counterfactual quality between high-resource and low-resource languages? Which is superior: the translation path or the direct generation path?

**Goal**: (1) Evaluate the quality of counterfactuals generated by LLMs across six languages via direct generation and translation; (2) Analyze the similarity of edits across languages; (3) Identify error types in multilingual counterfactuals; (4) Assess the effectiveness of multilingual counterfactual data augmentation.

**Key Insight**: The authors select six languages (English, Arabic, German, Spanish, Hindi, Swahili), covering high-resource to low-resource settings and multiple writing systems. They conduct a comprehensive evaluation using three LLMs of different scales (Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma3-27B, Llama3.3-70B) on two multilingual datasets (XNLI, SIB200).

**Core Idea**: By systematically comparing the direct generation and translation-based generation paths, this work reveals the capability boundaries, error modes, and data augmentation effects of LLM multilingual counterfactual generation, providing an empirical foundation for multilingual interpretability research.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

The study adopts an existing one-shot Chain-of-Thought counterfactual generation method: (1) identify keywords in the input that influence model prediction; (2) find replacement words that lead to the target label; (3) generate the counterfactual by replacement. Building on this, two paths are designed: generating counterfactuals directly in the target language (DG-CFs), and generating in English first then translating to the target language (TB-CFs).

### Key Designs

1.  **Dual-Path Counterfactual Generation**:
    -   Function: Compare the effectiveness of two multilingual counterfactual acquisition strategies.
    -   Mechanism: DG-CFs directly execute the three-step generation process (keyword identification → find replacement → replacement generation) in the target language. TB-CFs first generate counterfactuals in English and then use the same LLM to translate them into the target language. English prompts are used to unify experimental conditions.
    -   Design Motivation: LLMs perform strongest in English; the translation path might leverage this advantage to improve counterfactual quality in other languages, though it introduces translation noise.

2.  **Multi-dimensional Automatic Evaluation Framework**:
    -   Function: Evaluate counterfactual quality across effectiveness, similarity, and fluency dimensions.
    -   Mechanism: Label Flip Rate (LFR) measures if the counterfactual successfully changes the model prediction, $$LFR = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{M}(\tilde{x}_i) \neq \mathcal{M}(x_i))$$; Textual Similarity (TS) calculates semantic similarity using multilingual SBERT; Perplexity (PPL) assesses fluency using mGPT-1.3B.
    -   Design Motivation: A single metric is insufficient for a comprehensive quality assessment, requiring a balanced measurement of effectiveness, minimal editing, and naturalness.

3.  **Cross-lingual Editing Similarity Analysis**:
    -   Function: Quantify the degree of similarity in editing patterns among counterfactuals in different languages.
    -   Mechanism: Pairwise cosine similarity is calculated between counterfactuals of different languages using multilingual SBERT. Additionally, non-English counterfactuals are back-translated into English before calculating similarity to eliminate linguistic variance.
    -   Design Motivation: To verify whether LLMs follow similar editing strategies across different languages and reveal common principles of cross-lingual perturbations.

### Loss & Training

This paper does not involve model training for generation but instead uses existing LLMs for zero-shot/few-shot counterfactual generation. In counterfactual data augmentation (CDA) experiments, multilingual BERT is fine-tuned, categorized into cross-lingual CDA (using only English training data + counterfactuals) and multilingual CDA (using all language training data + counterfactuals).

**Translated Counterfactuals (TB-CFs) vs. Direct Generation**: TB-CFs achieve higher LFR in most cases, but the similarity is lower by 15.44% on average, and perplexity is higher by 38% on average.

### Key Findings

- English counterfactuals achieve the highest overall LFR, but they are not necessarily optimal in terms of fluency and edit distance—the "optimal language" depends on the specific metric.
- European languages (English, German, Spanish) show highly similar counterfactual edit patterns, while Arabic and Swahili exhibit significantly different patterns.
- Among the four error types, copy-paste is the most prevalent (avg. 6.7% for SIB200), and language confusion is more severe in low-resource languages.
- Multilingual CDA generally outperforms cross-lingual CDA, with the most significant improvement for Arabic (avg. +64.45%), but it is nearly ineffective for Swahili.

## Highlights & Insights

- This is the first systematic evaluation of LLM multilingual counterfactual generation capabilities, filling a critical gap in counterfactual explanations from English to a multilingual context.
- The error taxonomy (copy-paste, negation, inconsistency, language confusion) provides practical value and directions for future improvements.
- The study identifies an interesting "translation path: higher LFR but lower quality" trade-off—a higher label flip rate does not necessarily equate to a better counterfactual.

## Limitations & Future Work

- Only English prompts were used; the potential for target language prompting to improve results was not explored.
- The counterfactual generation method is relatively basic (one-shot CoT); more advanced methods might behave differently.
- CDA performance in low-resource languages like Swahili remains poor, requiring strategies specifically tailored for low-resource scenarios.
- Evaluation relied primarily on automatic metrics, with limited coverage in human evaluation.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs. ZeroCF/FIZLE**: These methods only evaluate English; this paper extends them to six languages, revealing new challenges in multilingual scenarios.
- **vs. Multilingual CDA (Liu et al., 2021)**: The former focused on CDA for machine translation, whereas this paper focuses on CDA for counterfactual explanations.
- **Insight**: Cross-lingual edit similarity analysis can inspire future research into multilingual alignment and cross-lingual transfer.
