# Physics-informed learning under mixing: How physical knowledge speeds up learning

**Conference**: ICLR 2026  
**OpenReview**: [https://openreview.net/forum?id=IvLVPbeoRx](https://openreview.net/forum?id=IvLVPbeoRx)  
**Code**: To be confirmed  
**Area**: Learning Theory / Statistical Learning  
**Keywords**: Physics-informed regularization, dependent data, excess risk bound, Sobolev minimax rate, martingale offset complexity

## TL;DR
Ours derives high-probability and expectation bounds for excess risk of "empirical risk minimization with physics-informed regularization" under **non-i.i.d. (dependent/mixing) data**. It proves that as long as the physical prior is "aligned" with the ground truth ($\|D(f^\star)\|_{L^2}^2\simeq 0$), the learning rate accelerates from the slow Sobolev minimax rate $O(T^{-d})$ to the $O(1/T)$ rate consistent with the i.i.d. optimal rate, **without losing effective sample size due to data dependence**.

## Background & Motivation
**Background**: Physics-informed machine learning injects known physical laws—typically partial differential equations (PDEs)—into learning algorithms. A common practice is adding a "physics consistency" regularization term to the loss or treating it as a constraint. Extensive empirical work (e.g., PINNs) shows this significantly improves data efficiency, generalization, and interpretability, which is vital for downstream safety-critical control.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: While the empirical utility of physical priors is well-recognized, **"how much and why" they help remains theoretically unclear**. Existing statistical learning analyses almost exclusively assume i.i.d. data, whereas real-world data often originate from dynamical trajectories $X_{t+1}=f^\star(X_t)+W_t$—where consecutive samples are strongly correlated.

**Key Challenge**: The classic approach to handling dependent data is "blocking"—partitioning a trajectory of length $T$ into $k$ blocks and treating them as approximately independent. However, this shrinks the effective sample size from $T$ to $T/k$, slowing the convergence rate. Nagaraj et al. (2020) proved that this "sample shrinkage" is **unavoidable** in the worst case. In short, dependent data naturally leads to slower rates, and whether physical priors can recover this has not been quantified.

**Goal**: Within a statistical learning framework, derive **complexity-dependent** excess risk bounds for regularized ERM—specifically, how the bound scales with $\|D(f^\star)\|_{L^2}$ (the deviation between prior and truth)—and characterize the resulting convergence rates.

**Key Insight**: The authors extend Mendelson's small-ball method and the localized analysis of non-linear dependent data by Ziemann & Tu (2022) to weighted, vector-valued Sobolev spaces with regularization by elliptic PDE operators. The critical intuition is that physical regularization does more than just shrink the hypothesis space; it allows the "complexity term" in the analysis to evade dependence on the mixing coefficients.

**Core Idea**: Construct a regularization term $\Psi(f)=\|D(f)\|_{L^2}^2$ using an elliptic operator $D$ that encodes the PDE. Prove that under "knowledge alignment," the cost incurred by dependent data is **entirely shifted into a one-time burn-in period**, thereby allowing the main term of the excess risk to recover the i.i.d. rate of $O(1/T)$.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
This is a pure theory paper. The "method" refers to a **proof chain**: starting from a regularized optimization problem, it gradually replaces "excess risk (difficult to analyze directly)" with "controllable complexity measures," finally optimizing free parameters to obtain convergence rates. The objective is to determine the convergence rate of $\|\hat f-f^\star\|_{L^2}^2$ under data dependence and how physical priors alter it.

The learning problem is set as follows: data consists of a trajectory of length $T$, $\mathcal D=\{X_t,Y_t\}_{t=0}^{T-1}$, generated by a non-linear dynamical system $Y_t=X_{t+1}=f^\star(X_t)+W_t$, where $W_t$ is a martingale difference sequence with respect to the trajectory σ-algebra filtration and is conditionally sub-Gaussian (variance proxy $\sigma_W^2$). The estimator is given by regularized ERM:

$$\hat f=\arg\min_{f\in F}\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|Y_t-f(X_t)\|_2^2+\lambda_T\,\Psi(f),$$

The hypothesis space $F$ is a ball of radius $\rho_f$ in a weighted, vector-valued Sobolev space $H^s$ ($s\ge 2d_X$), assuming $f^\star\in F$ (otherwise, a deterministic bias term independent of data would arise, which can be handled by decoupling).

The proof chain progresses through four steps: ① Encode the physical prior as a regularization term $\Psi$ using an elliptic PDE operator $D$; ② Use a one-sided concentration inequality to upper bound the excess risk ($L^2$ norm) by its empirical version on a critical sphere $\partial B(r)$; ③ Further bound the empirical version using the **martingale offset complexity** $M_T[F^\rho]$ of an effective hypothesis class, utilizing "self-normalization" to make this complexity **independent of mixing**; ④ Introduce S-persistence and $(C,\alpha)$-hypercontractivity to bound the probability of the "lower isometry event," thereby pushing the cost of data dependence into the burn-in time. Finally, by optimizing the critical radius $r$, the effective class radius $\rho$, and the regularization parameter $\lambda_T$, a bound is obtained that contains both a "slow Sobolev term" and a "fast i.i.d. term."

### Key Designs

**1. Encoding Physical Prior as Elliptic Operator Regularization**

The limitation was that the "usefulness of physical priors" could not be captured by analytical tools. Ours formalizes physical knowledge by assuming $f^\star$ approximately satisfies a PDE induced by a linear differential operator $D:H^s\to L^2$. The operator components are $[D(f)]_i=\sum_{|\alpha|\le s}p_{i,\alpha}D^\alpha f_i$ with bounded coefficients $p_{i,\alpha}$. The regularization term is the squared $L^2$ norm of this operator residual:

$$\Psi(f)=\|D(f)\|_{L^2(\Omega^T,P_X;\mathbb R^{d_Y})}^2,$$

**Knowledge alignment** is defined as $\|D(f^\star)\|_{L^2}^2\simeq 0$—meaning the ground truth "almost strictly" satisfies the PDE. Two technical choices are key: first, requiring $D$ to be an **elliptic** operator (Assumption 4: $\sum_{|\alpha|=s}p_{i,\alpha}\xi^\alpha\ne 0$), which generalizes Laplace/Poisson operators and ensures the regularization provides enough "rigidity" to suppress complexity. Second, $\Psi$ is proven to be a *2-proper* functional in the sense of Lecué & Mendelson, allowing the application of complexity-dependent analysis.

**2. One-sided Concentration + Lower Isometry Event**

Directly analyzing $L^2$ excess risk is difficult because the underlying probability measure is unknown. Following the small-ball approach of Mendelson (2014), a high-probability event is constructed such that for some parameter $\theta$:

$$\|f-f^\star\|_{L^2}^2\le\frac{\theta}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_t)-f^\star(X_t)\|_2^2.$$

This **one-sided concentration inequality** bounds the "theoretical norm" by the "empirical norm," shifting the analysis from unobservable $L^2$ risk to tractable empirical risk. The failure set is defined as the **lower isometry event** $A_r$, with its probability controlled by the critical radius $r$. Under dependent data, the i.i.d. small-ball condition is not directly applicable; Ours uses $(C,\alpha)$-hypercontractivity with $\alpha=2$ to rebuild the small-ball property on the critical sphere $\partial B(r)=\{f\in F:\|f-f^\star\|_{L^2}^2=r^2\}$. This step is the fulcrum of the proof: it converts the estimation accuracy problem into a problem of how large the empirical loss is on the critical sphere.

**3. Martingale Offset Complexity + Self-Normalization**

The empirical risk side requires a measure of "how complex the effective hypothesis class is." Ours uses **martingale offset complexity**: for each $f$ in the effective class $F^\rho_\star$ (shifted to be centered at $f^\star$):

$$\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_t)\|_2^2\ \le\ \sup_{f\in F^\rho_\star}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Big(4\langle W_t,f(X_t)\rangle_2-\|f(X_t)\|_2^2\Big)\ \overset{.}{=}\ M_T[F^\rho_\star].$$

The subtle brilliance lies in the $-\|f(X_t)\|_2^2$ "offset" term: it induces a **self-normalization effect** that exactly cancels the fluctuations of the noise inner product $\langle W_t,f(X_t)\rangle_2$ (inspired by Liang et al. 2015). This cancellation makes the martingale offset complexity **independent of data mixing**—the complexity itself does not grow due to sample correlation. This is the core mechanism for achieving fast rates, contrasting sharply with "blocking" which discounts the effective sample size.

**4. S-persistence + Hypercontractivity: Shifting Dependence to Burn-in Time**

The remaining task is to quantify the "probability that the lower isometry event fails," which is linked to the data dependency structure. Ours uses two regularity assumptions for dependent data: **S-persistence** (Assumption 6, a one-sided exponential inequality where larger $S\in[1,\infty)$ indicates stronger dependence on the past) and $(C,\alpha)$-**hypercontractivity**. By decomposing the expected excess risk via $A_r$ and its complement, and using S-persistence and hypercontractivity, the failure probability is suppressed into a term that decays exponentially with $T$: $\sim B^2 N_\infty(\partial B(r),r/\sqrt\theta)\exp(-8T/(\theta^2 C_r S))$. The significance is: **data dependence ($S$) only appears in the exponential term, affecting only the "burn-in sample size" required, without polluting the main term of the excess risk**.

### Loss & Training
The optimization objective is the regularized ERM (Eq 3.3): squared loss for data fitting + $\lambda_T\Psi(f)$. The theory provides a **lower bound for the regularization parameter**; for example, the expectation bound requires $\lambda_T\ge\frac{4(C_I+C_{II})(\sigma_W^2)^d}{3T\,\Psi(f^\star)^{1-d'/2}}$ and an effective class radius $\rho\ge 10\,\Psi(f^\star)$. These conditions essentially find a balance, determined by $T$, between "sufficient regularization to suppress complexity" and "avoiding excessive bias."

This is a pure theory paper with **no numerical experiments**. Its "key data" consists of theoretical rate comparisons and the rates provided by the main theorems.

### Key Findings
- **Data dependence is "moved"**: The dependence parameter $S$ only enters the burn-in time condition (lower bound on $T$) and does not infect the asymptotic main term of the excess risk—a fundamental breakthrough over "blocking" methods.
- **Acceleration comes from alignment, not regularization alone**: The slow term only disappears when $\Psi(f^\star)\simeq 0$ (prior consistent with truth); if the prior is biased, the slow Sobolev rate $T^{-d}$ remains.
- **First complexity-dependent bound in a probabilistic sense**: Compared to Doumèche et al. (2024) (i.i.d. + expectation only), Ours covers non-i.i.d. data and provides high-probability bounds.

## Highlights & Insights
- **Self-normalization is the key to beating mixing**: The offset term in martingale complexity allows the noise fluctuations to cancel out, decoupling complexity from mixing coefficients—a mechanism that could be adapted to other dependent data analyses.
- **Complexity-dependent bounds quantify prior quality into the rate**: The bound explicitly scales with $\|D(f^\star)\|_{L^2}$, providing a ruler: the more accurate the physical prior, the faster the rate and the shorter the burn-in.
- **Portable framework for Weighted Sobolev + Ellipticity**: To support the analysis, the authors generalized scalar Sobolev embedding, hypercontractivity, and entropy estimation to weighted vector-valued spaces, providing a ready-made toolbox for other "PDE constraint + learning" theoretical tasks.

## Limitations & Future Work
- **Acceleration at the cost of longer burn-in**: Stronger dependence ($S$) or higher dimensions ($d_X$) increase the minimum $T$ required for the theorem to take effect; acceleration may not be visible in small-sample or strong-dependence scenarios.
- **Strong assumption chain**: Relies on ellipticity, $s\ge 2d_X$, bounded densities, $f^\star\in F$, and constant $S$. If $f^\star\notin F$, an additional deterministic bias term would appear.
- **Lack of numerical validation**: All conclusions are asymptotic rates and constant dependencies; there is no experiment on a toy PDE system to demonstrate the actual length of burn-in or how alignment impacts finite-sample behavior.
- **Future Directions**: Derive computable upper bounds for $C_{\text{slow}}/C_{\text{fast}}$ and burn-in time; relax alignment to "partial alignment" and characterize the rate transition; extend to non-elliptic or time-varying operators.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs Blocking (Yu 1994; Sancetta 2021, etc.)**: Blocking yields suboptimal rates due to sample shrinkage. Ours avoids blocking and keeps the main term rate intact by shifting dependence to burn-in.
- **vs Ziemann & Tu (2022)**: Ours builds on their small-ball/offset localized analysis but is not a simple application; physics regularization introduces new challenges in characterizing entropy (ellipticity, non-trivial null spaces) and working in weighted vector-valued Sobolev spaces.
- **vs Doumèche et al. (2024)**: Closest in spirit, but they handle i.i.d. data and provide only expectation bounds. Ours advances to non-i.i.d. data and provides both expectation and high-probability results.
