# LexChronos: An Agentic Framework for Structured Event Timeline Extraction in Indian Jurisprudence

**Conference**: AAAI 2026
**arXiv**: [2603.01651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01651)  
**Code**: [https://github.com/Tummepalli/LexChronos](https://github.com/Tummepalli/LexChronos)  
**Area**: Model Compression
**Keywords**: Event Extraction, Legal Document Analysis, Indian Jurisprudence, Dual-Agent Framework, Timeline Extraction

## TL;DR

This paper proposes LexChronos, a dual-agent iterative framework for extracting structured event timelines from Indian Supreme Court judgments. A LoRA fine-tuned extraction agent identifies candidate events, while a pretrained feedback agent scores and refines them through a confidence-driven loop. The system achieves a BERT F1 of 0.8751 on a synthetic dataset, and the structured timelines are preferred by GPT-4 over unstructured baselines in 75% of downstream legal summarization cases.

## Background & Motivation

### State of the Field

AI applications in the global legal domain have moved from theory to practice, supporting tasks such as transcription, translation, and case management. LLMs are increasingly employed for legal research, drafting, and analysis. Nevertheless, extracting structured event timelines from court judgments remains an underexplored area.

### Limitations of Prior Work

**Unique challenges of the Indian judicial system**: Dense legal language, common law citations, constitutional references, and procedural dependencies make it difficult for conventional NLP techniques to capture the temporal, causal, and hierarchical structures in these documents.

**Sentence-level to document-level bottleneck**: While sentence-level event extraction has progressed, document-level extraction — requiring coreference resolution, cross-paragraph entity tracking, and temporal-causal association — remains a bottleneck.

**Absence of datasets**: No publicly available event-level annotated dataset for Indian Supreme Court judgments exists, hindering reproducibility and benchmarking.

**Limitations of LLMs**: Most approaches treat legal documents as monolithic text blocks, ignoring the fine-grained event structure that underpins legal reasoning.

### Root Cause

India operates one of the world's largest judicial systems (86,742 pending Supreme Court cases as of 2025), yet tools for structured analysis of its judgments are virtually nonexistent. Understanding and predicting judicial outcomes requires nuanced analysis of legal documents, and without structured representations, downstream tasks such as precedent mapping, argument generation, and judgment prediction cannot be effectively supported.

### Starting Point

The paper adopts a "reverse engineering" strategy to address the data scarcity problem: structured event timelines are first generated by LLMs, after which corresponding judgment texts are generated from those timelines, yielding a synthetic dataset. A dual-agent system is then trained on this dataset for iterative event extraction and quality refinement.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

LexChronos comprises two core components:

1. **Dataset construction pipeline**: Case category selection → event timeline generation → judgment text generation (reverse engineering)
2. **Dual-agent extraction framework**: Extraction agent (LoRA fine-tuned) → Feedback agent (pretrained) → Iterative refinement loop

### Key Designs

#### 1. LexChronos Event Schema (LES)

A four-attribute event schema is defined, covering eight core components of Indian Supreme Court judgments:

| Attribute | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Timestamp | Time or date of the event |
| Event | Narrative description of the event |
| Judge | Name of the presiding judge (if applicable) |
| Precedent | Legal precedents cited in the event |

**Schema validation**: The sufficiency of the four-attribute schema is validated by mapping it to the eight core judgment components (facts, issues, plaintiff arguments, defendant arguments, legal analysis, precedent analysis, court reasoning, and conclusion) — each component can be covered by some combination of LES attributes.

- **Design Motivation**: The four-attribute schema represents the minimal sufficient representation derived through inductive analysis of judgment documents, capable of capturing all substantive legal content while maintaining machine-readable conciseness.

#### 2. Synthetic Dataset Construction (Reverse Engineering Pipeline)

A three-step process:

**Step 1 — Case category selection**: Categories are randomly sampled from the 25 official Supreme Court case classifications (covering criminal law, civil law, cyber law, intellectual property, etc.).

**Step 2 — Event timeline generation**: DeepSeek-R1 and GPT-4 generate structured timelines conditioned on the case category:
$$E = \text{Prompt}_E(\text{case category})$$

**Step 3 — Judgment text generation**: Simulated judgment texts are generated with the timeline as a constraint:
$$J = \text{Prompt}_J(E)$$

The final dataset is $D = \{(E_i, J_i)\}_{i=1}^{2000}$, with 1,000 samples each from DeepSeek-R1 and GPT-4.

A comparison of the two models reveals that DeepSeek-R1 produces longer judgments (average 1,011 words vs. 553), more events (27 vs. 19), richer precedents (6 vs. 3), and a larger vocabulary (34,623 vs. 17,080).

- **Design Motivation**: Due to the scarcity of annotated legal data, the reverse engineering strategy — generating annotations before generating text — ensures consistency between annotations and text.

#### 3. Dual-Agent Iterative Extraction

**Extraction agent**: A LoRA fine-tuned small LLM (<4B parameters) that extracts candidate events from judgment text:
$$E_0 = \text{ExtractionAgent}(J)$$

**Feedback agent**: A pretrained LLM (<4B parameters) that evaluates extraction results and provides feedback:
$$F_0 = \text{FeedbackAgent}(J, E_0)$$

**Iterative refinement**: The extraction agent refines its output based on feedback, and the feedback agent re-evaluates:
$$E_{i+1} = \text{ExtractionAgent}(J, E_i, F_i)$$
$$F_{i+1} = \text{FeedbackAgent}(J, E_{i+1})$$

**Stopping conditions** (either of the following):
- **Patience limit**: Confidence score fails to exceed the historical best $S_{\text{best}}$ for 3 consecutive iterations
- **Tolerance threshold**: Confidence score remains identical for 3 consecutive iterations

**Seven-dimensional feedback scoring** (0.00–1.00): Narrative relevance, temporal accuracy, chronological fluency, event detail, redundancy detection, role identification, and confidence score.

- **Mechanism**: Metacognitive feedback ensures semantic coherence and factual accuracy, converging toward high-confidence timelines.
- **Design Motivation**: Single-pass extraction has limited quality; iterative refinement progressively improves results. Separating extraction and evaluation roles mitigates self-assessment bias.

### Loss & Training

- Extraction agent: LoRA fine-tuning on 1,600 training samples, evaluated on 400 test samples
- Feedback agent: Pretrained model used as-is, with no additional training
- Prompting strategy: Zero-shot prompting with role prompts and style prompts
- Evaluation metric: BERT-based F1 (rather than ROUGE/BLEU), as subtle semantic distinctions in legal language are more important than token-level matching

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

**Extraction agent performance across eight candidate models**:

| Model | BERT Precision | BERT Recall | BERT F1 |
|-------|---------------|-------------|---------|
| **Llama 3.2 3B Instruct** | **0.8450** | **0.8336** | **0.8390** |
| Gemma 3 1B IT | 0.8237 | 0.7992 | 0.8113 |
| Gemma 2 2B IT | 0.7809 | 0.7957 | 0.7880 |
| Llama 3.2 1B Instruct | 0.6058 | 0.6054 | 0.6052 |
| DeepSeek R1 Distill Qwen 1.5B | 0.5548 | 0.5135 | 0.5276 |
| Qwen 2.5 3B | 0.5628 | 0.4467 | 0.4752 |
| Phi 4 Mini Reasoning | 0.4802 | 0.2442 | 0.3048 |
| Qwen 3 4B | 0.4472 | 0.2506 | 0.3005 |

**Effect of feedback agent on extraction quality**:

| Feedback Configuration | BERT Precision | BERT Recall | BERT F1 |
|------------------------|---------------|-------------|---------|
| Self-feedback (Llama 3.2 3B fine-tuned) | 0.8681 | 0.8371 | 0.8523 |
| **Gemma 2 2B IT** | **0.8918** | **0.8590** | **0.8751** |
| Gemma 3 1B IT | 0.8664 | 0.8348 | 0.8502 |
| Llama 3.2 3B Instruct | 0.8665 | 0.8351 | 0.8505 |
| Llama 3.2 1B Instruct | 0.8661 | 0.8344 | 0.8499 |

### Ablation Study

**Effect of iterative refinement (implicit ablation)**:
- No feedback (single-pass extraction): F1 = 0.8390
- Self-feedback: F1 = 0.8523 (+1.33%)
- **Optimal feedback (Gemma 2 2B): F1 = 0.8751 (+3.61%)**

**Downstream task — Legal summarization (GPT-4 preference evaluation, 200 samples)**:

| Summarization Model | Prefer Unstructured Input | Prefer Structured Timeline Input |
|--------------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Llama 3.1 8B Instruct | 55 | **145 (72.5%)** |
| Gemma 2 9B IT | 50 | **150 (75.0%)** |

### Key Findings

1. **Llama 3.2 3B is the optimal extraction agent**: Achieves the highest F1 (0.8390) among 8 candidate models; models with fewer parameters or reasoning-oriented designs (e.g., Phi 4 Mini Reasoning) perform markedly worse.
2. **Heterogeneous feedback outperforms self-feedback**: Using a different model (Gemma 2 2B) as the feedback agent (F1 = 0.8751) surpasses self-feedback (F1 = 0.8523), demonstrating the value of an external perspective.
3. **Gains from iterative refinement**: Three iterations represent the optimal trade-off between performance and computational cost.
4. **Structured representations substantially improve downstream tasks**: A 75% preference rate indicates that event timelines function as a "knowledge preprocessor," enabling summarization models to focus on generation rather than comprehension.
5. **BERT metrics are preferable to ROUGE/BLEU**: In the legal domain, semantic equivalence is more meaningful than token-level matching.

## Highlights & Insights

1. **Reverse engineering for dataset construction**: Generating annotations before generating text elegantly addresses the scarcity of annotated data in the legal domain.
2. **Conciseness of the four-attribute event schema**: The four attributes — Timestamp, Event, Judge, and Precedent — are sufficient to cover all core components of a judgment.
3. **Separation of extraction and feedback roles**: The fine-tuned extraction agent handles generation while the pretrained feedback agent handles evaluation, with clearly delineated responsibilities.
4. **A practical pipeline for legal AI**: The system forms a complete loop from dataset construction to extraction, evaluation, and downstream application.
5. **Effective use of small models**: All components use models with fewer than 4B parameters, making the framework friendly to resource-constrained legal AI deployments.

## Limitations & Future Work

1. **Representativeness of the synthetic dataset**: Although structural fidelity is validated, synthetic judgments may differ from real judgments in linguistic complexity and argumentative depth.
2. **Coverage limited to the Indian Supreme Court**: Judgments from lower courts, arbitration tribunals, and special tribunals may have different structural conventions.
3. **GPT-4 as the sole evaluator**: Downstream summarization evaluation relies entirely on GPT-4 preferences, without human assessment.
4. **Event granularity**: The four-attribute schema may lack sufficient granularity for certain cases, such as complex multi-party or cross-claim litigation.
5. **Cross-lingual challenges**: India has multiple official languages; the current system handles only English judgments.
6. **Stopping condition configuration**: The choice of patience limit = 3 lacks sufficient justification and may result in premature stopping or unnecessary iterations.
7. **Integration with existing legal NER systems is not discussed**: For example, the complementarity with Kalamkar et al.'s fine-grained 14-category entity recognition system is not addressed.

## Related Work & Insights

- **AALAP** (Tiwari 2024): Mistral 7B fine-tuned for legal tasks, but underperforms on summarization and constitutional QA → this paper focuses on event extraction instead.
- **DLEE**: The first Chinese legal document-level event extraction dataset → inspired the creation of an analogous resource for Indian law.
- **Legal Sarathi**: A legal event extraction system integrating LLMs and ML → employs a RAG framework but does not adopt iterative refinement.
- **Self-Refine** (Madaan et al.): An iterative self-feedback framework → this paper finds that heterogeneous feedback outperforms self-feedback.
- **Insight**: In specialized domains with scarce annotated data, reverse-engineering-based synthetic data construction may represent a general-purpose solution.
