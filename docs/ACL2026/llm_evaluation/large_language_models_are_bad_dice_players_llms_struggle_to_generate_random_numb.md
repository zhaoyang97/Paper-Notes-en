# Large Language Models Are Bad Dice Players: LLMs Struggle to Generate Random Numbers from Statistical Distributions

**Conference**: ACL 2026  
**arXiv**: [2601.05414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05414)  
**Code**: [GitHub](https://github.com/Mininda/LLM_Bad_Dice_Player)  
**Area**: Image Generation  
**Keywords**: Probability Sampling, Random Number Generation, Distribution Fidelity, LLM Intrinsic Capability, Downstream Bias

## TL;DR

This paper presents the first large-scale systematic audit of the native sampling capabilities of 11 frontier LLMs across 15 probability distributions. It reveals that LLMs severely lack intrinsic probability sampling mechanisms, and this deficiency translates into systematic biases in downstream applications.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: LLMs are evolving from simple dialogue interfaces into core components of complex application pipelines, including synthetic data generation, Agent simulation, educational material construction, and text-to-image prompt synthesis. These scenarios increasingly require LLMs to faithfully sample from specified probability distributions.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Existing studies have sporadically identified biases in LLMs during simple random generation tasks—such as preferences for "lucky numbers" or biases in coin flipping. However, these studies are limited by small sample sizes ($N=100$), a narrow range of distributions (5 types), and single sampling protocols, failing to comprehensively evaluate the native sampling capabilities of LLMs. Current practices rely on external libraries (e.g., `numpy.random`) to generate distribution-compliant data, a "workaround" that implies a lack of underlying functional capability in LLMs.

**Key Challenge**: If LLMs aiming for general intelligence cannot faithfully sample even basic probability distributions, they will introduce uncontrollable systematic biases in downstream applications requiring statistical guarantees. However, there is a lack of large-scale, statistically valid benchmarks to verify this hypothesis.

**Goal**: To conduct the first large-scale probability sampling audit of frontier LLMs. **Key Insight**: Design a dual-protocol experimental framework (Batch vs. Independent) to decouple different failure modes. **Core Idea**: LLMs lack functional internal samplers; batch generation functions marginally through context dependency, while independent requests fail almost entirely.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

This work does not propose a new model but builds an audit pipeline to answer "whether LLMs possess intrinsic probability samplers." It tasks 11 frontier LLMs to generate $N$ samples each for 15 probability distributions (categorized into three tiers by complexity) using two sampling protocols. The deviation between generated and target distributions is quantified using three metrics: Wasserstein-1 distance $\mathcal{W}_1$, KL divergence, and statistical tests (KS/χ²). The input consists of instructions to "sample from a certain distribution," the intermediate stage is the sequence of samples generated by the model, and the output is the fidelity score per distribution and protocol. Two additional downstream experiments (MCQ option randomization and T2I demographic attribute prompts) connect this deficiency to real-world applications, verifying that biases are transmitted directly.

### Key Designs

**1. Dual-Protocol Design (Batch vs. Independent): Separating "Contextual Success" from "Intrinsic Priors"**

Previous research used only one protocol, making it impossible to determine whether an LLM's apparent sampling ability stemmed from a true internal sampler or merely from self-correction by looking back at historical samples. This paper isolates these capabilities using two complementary protocols: Protocol A (Batch Generation) produces $N=1000$ samples in a single response, allowing the model to dynamically adjust frequencies based on generated content; Protocol B (Independent Request) uses $N=1000$ stateless calls to produce 1 sample each, completely severing context and exposing only the model's intrinsic prior. The gap between the results of these two protocols serves as a diagnostic signal—marginal performance in batch mode versus near-total failure in independent mode indicates that sampling capability is parasitic on long-context dependencies rather than intrinsic mechanisms.

**2. Theoretical Characterization of the Context-Fidelity Dilemma: Explaining Why More Samples Lead to Lower Accuracy**

Standard Monte Carlo expected error converges at $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$ with the number of samples, yet LLMs exhibit an anomaly where $\mathcal{W}_1$ increases as $N$ increases. The authors explain this through an error decomposition: under independent requests, the expected error $\mathcal{E}(N) = \Delta_{\text{ind}} + \mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$ can only converge to an irreducible bias $\Delta_{\text{ind}}$, where increased sampling cannot eliminate the model's systematic preferences. In batch generation, the error is split into two opposing forces: Correction Gain (improvement from contextual self-correction) and Drift (degradation from accumulated drift in autoregressive generation). Once a sequence exceeds a critical length, Drift outweighs Correction Gain, causing longer sample streams to fit less accurately. This framework grounds the counter-intuitive non-monotonic curves observed in experiments into an explainable mechanism.

**3. Three-Tier Distribution Complexity Classification: Testing if Fidelity Degrades Monotonically with Difficulty**

To systematically identify where performance collapses, the authors organized 15 distributions into three tiers based on entropy characteristics, support constraints, and tail behavior: Tier I includes basic distributions (Uniform, Gaussian, Bernoulli); Tier II includes bounded/discrete distributions (Beta, Binomial, Poisson, Exponential); Tier III includes heavy-tailed/multi-parameter distributions (Cauchy, t, Chi-squared, F, Gamma, Weibull, Laplace, Logistic). This hierarchy transforms the vague notion that "sampling fidelity degrades with complexity" into a quantifiable monotonic trend; in experiments, $\mathcal{W}_1$ rises from approximately 0.1 in Tier I to approximately 1.5 in Tier III, clearly marking the boundaries of capability.

### Evaluation Metrics & Protocol
As an evaluation-focused work, no training is involved. Statistical tests (KS/χ², $\alpha=0.01$) serve as binary "pass/fail" diagnostics, $\mathcal{W}_1$ acts as a continuous fidelity measure, and KL divergence serves as an information loss metric. All models utilize default decoding parameters ($T{=}1.0$, top-p${=}1.0$) to reflect out-of-the-box scenarios.

### Key Findings
- A **sharp protocol asymmetry** exists between batch generation and independent requests: effective sampling relies on long-context dependencies rather than intrinsic capabilities.
- Sampling fidelity degrades monotonically with distribution complexity; $\mathcal{W}_1$ rises from ~0.1 in Tier I to ~1.5 in Tier III.
- Larger sampling budgets $N$ lead to poorer distribution fitting, violating the standard $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$ convergence expectation.
- In downstream applications, LLMs fail to follow explicit distribution constraint instructions; MCQ position bias and demographic attribute bias are pervasive and severe.

## Highlights & Insights
- The dual-protocol design is a key methodological innovation, refining the sampling problem from "can it approximate" to "does it depend on context or intrinsic capability."
- The theoretical framework of the Context-Fidelity Dilemma elegantly explains non-monotonic behavior in batch generation.
- Downstream application experiments (MCQ, T2I prompts) directly link abstract sampling issues to practical application risks, enhancing the paper's impact.
- The conclusion is clear: current LLMs lack functional internal samplers and require external tools to provide statistical guarantees.
- The three-tier distribution complexity classification provides a reusable evaluation framework that can be extended to more distribution families in the future.

## Limitations & Future Work
- Only default decoding parameters ($T{=}1.0$, top-p${=}1.0$) were tested; different decoding strategies (e.g., low temperature, top-k) might yield different performance.
- Enhancement strategies such as Chain-of-Thought or code generation were not explored as means to improve sampling capability, though these are common workarounds in practice.
- The study covers only 1D distributions; multivariate joint distributions are significantly more complex (preliminary experiments on Bivariate Gaussian are reported in the appendix).
- Future research could investigate whether specialized fine-tuning, RLHF, or specific training objectives could grant LLMs intrinsic sampling capabilities.
- The scale and scenarios of downstream experiments can be further expanded to more practical application domains.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. Gu et al. (2024)**: Previous comprehensive research only covered 5 distributions × $N=100$ × single protocol; this paper significantly scales up and deepens the methodology.
- **vs. Hopkins et al. (2023)**: Identified "lucky number" preferences but limited to uniform distribution integers; this paper systematically covers 15 continuous and discrete distributions.
- **vs. Xiao et al. (2025)**: Revealed Bernoulli sampling bias; this paper extends to continuous distributions and adds downstream application verification.
