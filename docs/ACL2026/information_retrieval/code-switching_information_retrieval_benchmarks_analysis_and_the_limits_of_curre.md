# Code-Switching Information Retrieval: Benchmarks, Analysis, and the Limits of Current Retrievers

**Conference**: ACL 2026  
**arXiv**: [2604.17632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17632)  
**Code**: GitHub + HuggingFace Dataset (Available)  
**Area**: Information Retrieval / Multilingual  
**Keywords**: Code-switching, Multilingual Retrieval, MTEB Benchmark, Vocabulary Expansion, Embedding Space

## TL;DR
This paper systematically evaluates the impact of "code-switching queries" on modern IR systems for the first time. It introduces the human-annotated CSR-L benchmark and an 11-task CS-MTEB suite generated by LLMs. The study reveals that even strong 8B multilingual models experience a drop of 4–13 nDCG@10 points under query-side code-switching, while rerankers can plummet from 60 to 25. Furthermore, it demonstrates that lexicon-based vocabulary expansion alleviates but fails to close the gap with monolingual baselines.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Modern IR has evolved from BM25 to dense bi-encoders (e5, bge-m3, Arctic-Embed, Qwen3-Embedding), cross-encoder rerankers, and late-interaction architectures like ColBERT v2. Benchmarks such as MTEB, BEIR, and MMTEB have expanded evaluation to hundreds of languages and tasks.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Existing benchmarks assume queries are monolingual. However, approximately 70% of the global population is bilingual. Bing logs show that code-switched queries account for ~27% in entertainment domains (Gupta 2014), and "English technical terms + framing in native language" queries are extremely common in practical retrieval scenarios. The effectiveness of the entire IR stack in this context has not been systematically tested.

**Key Challenge**: Embedding models use contrastive learning to push semantically similar sentences into the same vector space. Code-switching simultaneously introduces two token distributions into a single query, which may "split" the query representation, causing it to fall into unrelated subspaces. Additionally, uneven tokenizer coverage causes code-switched queries to be fragmented into numerous low-frequency subwords, further increasing representation noise.

**Goal**: (1) Construct an IR benchmark reflecting natural code-switching habits (human-annotated); (2) Expand evaluation to larger scales and diverse task types to examine if the impact of code-switching is limited to retrieval; (3) Determine if a low-cost "vocabulary expansion" intervention can mitigate the performance gap.

**Key Insight**: Instead of constructing adversarial queries, the authors task bilingual native speakers with rewriting queries based on real search behavior while preserving information needs. Meanwhile, PCA visualization of query representations in embedding space is used to answer the mechanistic question of "why models fail," rather than merely reporting performance degradation.

**Core Idea**: Treat code-switching as a long-neglected query distribution shift. Quantify the phenomenon using natural annotation and large-scale LLM generation via two complementary benchmarks, then use vocabulary expansion for mechanism probing to highlight "code-switching" as a critical weakness in IR robustness.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
The methodology consists of a three-stage evaluation and intervention pipeline rather than a new model:

1.  **CSR-L Benchmark Construction**: Four representative English IR datasets (Touché 2020 / HumanEval / TRECCOVID / FollowIR) are selected. Three multilingual authors (Chinese-English-Japanese) rewrite queries into Chinese/Japanese code-switched versions in a two-step process (rewriting and verification) covering four task types.
2.  **CS-MTEB Large-scale Extension**: MiMo-V2-Flash is used to rewrite queries for 11 MTEB tasks (covering 7 categories: instruction reranking, retrieval, clustering, classification, STS, reranking, and pair classification) into 9 languages × English code-switched versions. Prompt templates are iteratively optimized using CSR-L human-written ground truths, supported by 50 manual spot-checks.
3.  **Vocabulary Expansion Intervention**: For English-centric models (e.g., all-MiniLM-L12-v2, e5-large-v2), a bilingual lexicon (Conneau 2018) maps target word $w_t$ to the average subword embedding of the source word $w_s$: $v_{w_s}=\frac{1}{|T(w_s)|}\sum_{k\in T(w_s)} e_k$. These are aggregated into the target token embedding $e_{w_t}=\frac{1}{|N(w_t)|}\sum_{w_s\in N(w_t)} v_{w_s}$. Unmapped tokens are initialized with $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. The adapted models are then re-evaluated on CSR-L.

### Key Designs

1.  **Human + LLM Dual-track Benchmark (CSR-L + CS-MTEB)**:
    *   **Function**: Synergize "naturalness" and "scale" through two complementary benchmarks—CSR-L ensures linguistic naturalness, while CS-MTEB ensures breadth across tasks and languages.
    *   **Mechanism**: CSR-L strictly controls length variations (±20%), preserves entity/code tokens, and ensures both languages contribute content. CS-MTEB uses these rules as grounded prompt templates for large-scale replication with MiMo-V2-Flash.
    *   **Design Motivation**: There are no reliable automatic metrics for CS naturalness; thus, small-scale evaluation must be manual. Conversely, manual annotation for 11 tasks × 9 languages is infeasible, requiring LLM replication. This combination allows for both quantification (CSR-L) and generalization testing (CS-MTEB).

2.  **Cross-family Retriever Evaluation + Embedding Diagnosis**:
    *   **Function**: Locate commonalities of code-switching damage across IR paradigms and explain failure mechanisms via geometric diagnosis.
    *   **Mechanism**: Evaluation covers four paradigms: Statistical (BM25), Bi-encoder (e5/mE5/bge-m3/Arctic-Embed/Qwen3-Embedding), Cross-encoder (jina-reranker-v3, bge-reranker-v2-m3, Qwen3-Reranker), and Late-interaction (ColBERT v2). PCA is applied to original vs. code-switched query embeddings for e5-large-v2 and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B to quantify centroid distances.
    *   **Design Motivation**: Identifying a performance drop is insufficient; the paper aims to explain "why." Visualizations show that English-centric models cause original and code-switched queries to split into distinct clusters (centroid distance 0.25), whereas multilingual models show more overlap (0.20), correlating with the performance gap.

3.  **Vocabulary Expansion as a Mechanism Probe**:
    *   **Function**: Use a low-cost intervention to reveal whether code-switching failure is a tokenizer coverage issue or a deeper representation alignment problem.
    *   **Mechanism**: Target language token embeddings are initialized with source subword averages using word-level bilingual lexicons. The core model remains frozen; only the tokenizer and embedding layer are modified with zero additional training.
    *   **Design Motivation**: If vocabulary expansion closes the gap, the issue lies in the tokenizer; if it only partially alleviates it, the problem is deeper. Results show partial mitigation, proving that tokenization is part of the story, but code-switching remains a semantic challenge that cannot be solved by surface-level patches alone.

### Loss & Training
No new models are trained. Evaluation metrics include nDCG@10 (IR), p-MRR (FollowIR), V-measure (clustering), Accuracy (CLS), Cosine Spearman (STS), MAP@1000 (reranking), and mean AP (pair classification). Vocabulary expansion involves no gradient updates, only embedding initialization changes.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results (CSR-L English-Chinese, nDCG@10, p-MRR)

| Method Family | Model | Touché Orig | Touché CSR-L | TRECCOVID Orig | TRECCOVID CSR-L | Avg Drop |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statistical | BM25 | 60.32 | 37.68 | 55.62 | 46.43 | **−6.56** |
| Bi-encoder (EN) | e5-large-v2 | 42.52 | 22.88 | 66.64 | 50.42 | **−11.90** |
| Bi-encoder (EN) | all-MiniLM-L12-v2 | 49.22 | 23.85 | 51.17 | 39.51 | **−12.36** |
| Bi-encoder (Multilingual) | mE5-large | 49.32 | 42.75 | 71.56 | 56.54 | −6.90 |
| Bi-encoder (Multilingual) | Arctic-Embed-l-v2.0 | 64.05 | 54.91 | 83.63 | 76.99 | −4.54 |
| Bi-encoder (Multilingual) | Qwen3-Embedding-8B | 75.77 | 68.55 | 94.68 | 89.72 | **−3.68** |
| Cross-encoder | Qwen3-Reranker-8B | 40.91 | 32.01 | 84.58 | 69.88 | −6.67 |
| Late-interaction | ColBERT v2 | 61.62 | 29.30 | 69.30 | 53.74 | **−11.31** |

CS-MTEB (e5-large-v2) cross-lingual drops: Chinese −15.21, Japanese −12.70, German −12.27, Spanish −10.91. Reranking tasks are particularly vulnerable—e5-large-v2 drops from 60.17 to 25.75 under Japanese code-switching.

### Ablation Study (CSR-L-Chinese Average)

| Model | Original model + CSR-L-Chinese | Adapted model + CSR-L-Chinese | Gain | Gap to monolingual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all-MiniLM-L12-v2 | 30.09 | 37.73 | +7.64 | vs 42.45 Monolingual |
| e5-large-v2 | 35.32 | 43.50 | +8.18 | vs 47.22 Monolingual |

Gain for Japanese adapted models is smaller (all-MiniLM 29.94→34.34; e5-large-v2 34.70→39.80).

### Key Findings
- **Significant Gap Between EN-Centric and Multilingual Models**: e5-large-v2 drops 11.90 on average, whereas Qwen3-Embedding-8B drops only 3.68. Multilingual pre-training helps but is insufficient.
- **Limited Help from Model Scaling**: Improvements for Qwen3-Embedding from 0.6B (−4.74) to 4B (−4.39) to 8B (−3.68) are slow, indicating scaling does not solve the code-switching issue.
- **Dramatic Task Variation**: Reranking is most fragile (e5 Japanese: 60→25), while pair classification is more robust (>54), as reranking requires precise alignment.
- **Embedding Geometry Correlates with Performance**: Original and code-switched queries split into two clusters in PCA space for EN-centric models; multilingual models show more overlap.
- **Vocabulary Expansion is Partially Effective**: It recovers about half the gap, proving code-switching is not merely a tokenizer issue.

## Highlights & Insights
- **Brings "Code-Switching" into IR Robustness Research**: Quantifies the phenomenon with human and LLM benchmarks, identifying a significant weakness for future retrievers.
- **Combination of Geometric Diagnosis and Vocabulary Probing**: Answers "why models fail" and whether surface-level fixes work within a single study.
- **Task-Dependent Robustness Hypothesis**: Tasks requiring fine-grained ranking collapse under CS, while coarse semantic tasks are less affected, guiding future CS-aware reranker design (e.g., explicit token alignment loss).
- **CS-MTEB as a Reusable Asset**: Provides a ready-made stress test for multilingual robustness across 11 tasks and 9 languages.

## Limitations & Future Work
- Code-switching in this study is query-level and phrase-level; it does not cover romanization, transliteration, or document-level mixing.
- Human and LLM generation may involve minor style shifts despite quality control.
- Future Work: Implementing CS-aware contrastive learning in multilingual retrievers; replacing cross-lingual alignment with explicit token-level alignment; or incorporating "switch-point detection" as auxiliary supervision for rerankers.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. MMTEB / MTEB**: Standard multilingual benchmarks assume monolingual queries; CS-MTEB adds the code-switching dimension to the same task sets.
- **vs. MINERS / Litschko 2023**: Previous work focused on bitext mining or cross-dialectal tasks; this paper systematically covers four IR paradigms and 7 MTEB task categories.
- **vs. ContrastiveMix (Do 2024)**: While ContrastiveMix uses CS data for training, this paper establishes CS evaluation as an independent dimension.
- **vs. Zuo 2025**: Mirroring findings that LLM rerankers struggle with multilingual bi-encoder outputs, this study shows that LLM-based systems are broadly vulnerable to internal language mixing.
