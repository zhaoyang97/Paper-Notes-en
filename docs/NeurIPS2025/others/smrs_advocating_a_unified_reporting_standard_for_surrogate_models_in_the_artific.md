# SMRS: Advocating a Unified Reporting Standard for Surrogate Models in the Artificial Intelligence Era

**Conference**: NeurIPS 2025  
**arXiv**: [2502.06753](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06753)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: Scientific Modeling / Surrogate Models / Standardization Frameworks  
**Keywords**: Surrogate Model, Reporting Standard, Reproducibility, Uncertainty Quantification, Cross-domain Standardization

## TL;DR

Addressing the lack of standardized reporting protocols in AI-driven surrogate modeling, this paper proposes SMRS, a lightweight, modular, and model-agnostic reporting standard. SMRS spans six major dimensions of the entire modeling pipeline (including data collection, model selection, training methods, and evaluation metrics). The feasibility of this framework is validated through case studies on 17 published papers, aiming to enhance the reproducibility, comparability, and cross-domain transferability of surrogate models.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: As approximate replacements for expensive computational simulations or complex physical systems, surrogate models have become indispensable tools in engineering design, natural sciences, climate simulation, epidemiology, drug discovery, and many other fields. The core idea is to substitute an expensive original simulator or experimental system with a computationally cheaper model, thereby achieving orders-of-magnitude acceleration in scenarios such as optimization, design exploration, and uncertainty quantification. Traditional methods like Gaussian Processes, Splines, and Polynomial Chaos Expansion have long dominated the field. However, in recent years, they have been progressively supplemented or even surpassed by new AI-driven approaches: neural network surrogates can handle ultra-high-dimensional inputs and outputs; Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) directly embed differential equation constraints into network training; Bayesian Neural Networks offer native uncertainty estimation; and conditional deep generative models (such as variational autoencoders and diffusion models) generate complete conditional distributions rather than single-point predictions. More frontier paradigms, including World Models in reinforcement learning (which learn environmental state transitions to assist policy training) and Digital Twins (real-time digital mirrors of physical systems), are further blurring the boundary between surrogate models and simulators.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: The rapid emergence of AI methods has led to a highly fragmented research landscape. Each application domain utilizes its own customary modeling approaches, data formats, and evaluation protocols, creating numerous "isolated islands" that are difficult to compare. Specifically, inconsistency in key decisions is reflected at multiple levels: Is the model deterministic or probabilistic? Is prediction uncertainty quantified, and if so, how? How was the training data sampled, and what motivated the choice of this sampling scheme? What are the assumptions and applicability limits of the model? These questions are handled casually or ignored entirely in many publications. The authors conducted a systematic review of 17 recent surrogate modeling studies and discovered a startling fact: while approximately 90% of the papers detailed the model architecture and prediction accuracy (as these are the "selling points" of the papers), only about 35% described the data collection process, only about 25% explained the rationale behind the sampling design, the reporting rate for uncertainty quantification was around 30%, and analyses of limitations stood at only about 20%. This severe reporting incompleteness means that even if a paper demonstrates state-of-the-art prediction performance, readers still cannot judge whether the results were obtained on carefully curated favorable data, how much uncertainty the model exhibits in real deployment, and under what conditions the model might fail.

**Key Challenge**: The application of surrogate models is increasingly prevalent in high-risk areas such as climate change prediction, epidemic spread simulation, drug molecule design, and nuclear reactor safety assessment. In these scenarios, erroneous surrogate model predictions can lead to catastrophic decision failures. However, the lack of a unified reporting standard prevents researchers from effectively assessing the reliability and scope of a surrogate model, let alone conducting fair comparisons between different methods. This conflict is particularly prominent during cross-domain knowledge transfer: if a climate scientist wants to leverage the successful experience of using Seq2Seq surrogate models for Bayesian inference in epidemiology, she must painstakingly extract information about model architecture, training strategies, and evaluation methods from a paper filled with epidemiological terminology. Such critical information is often scattered throughout the paper, formatted and phrased differently from the climate domain, or simply not reported. The immense cost of cross-domain information extraction severely hinders the flow of scientific knowledge.

**Goal** This paper aims to answer a fundamental methodological question: How can a universal, flexible, and actionable standard for the development and reporting of surrogate models be established? This question can be decomposed into three specific sub-questions: (1) What key dimensions should surrogate model reporting cover? What specific information needs to be documented for each dimension? (2) How can a balance be struck between standardization and flexibility—neither restricting methodological innovations and domain-specific requirements nor compromising comparability across different papers? (3) Can this standard be equally applicable to both traditional statistical surrogates and modern AI-driven generative surrogates, achieving true "method-agnosticism"?

**Key Insight**: The authors' key insight is to reframe the problem from "method standardization" to "reporting standardization." Drawing from the successful experience of "Model Cards" (Mitchell et al., 2019) in the machine learning community—which have been widely adopted by Google, Hugging Face, Microsoft, etc., to significantly enhance model transparency by documenting training data, evaluation metrics, biases, and applicability in a structured format. The authors adapt the core philosophy of Model Cards to the surrogate modeling domain with extensive customization: surrogate modeling entails unique practices (such as the choice of experimental design/sampling strategies, multi-fidelity data fusion, embedding of physical laws, simulation-based inference, etc.) that are completely unaddressed in standard ML Model Cards. Meanwhile, referencing the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data management principles ensures the feasibility of progressive adoption.

**Core Idea**: Propose a modular "SMRS Report Card" standard to address fragmentation, opacity, and non-reproducibility in the surrogate modeling domain through standardized reporting rather than standardized methods.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

At the core of SMRS is a reporting schema covering the entire lifecycle of surrogate models. The input to the entire framework consists of all decisions and results from a surrogate model development project, and the output is a structured SMRS report card (recommended as a standard appendix to papers and repository attachments). The intermediate process collects information across six stages: first, the pre-assessment stage—evaluating whether a surrogate model is genuinely needed; then, the data stage—documenting data sources, sampling designs, and data formats; next, the modeling stage—recording the choice of model type and its motivation; followed by the training stage—documenting learning methods and optimization strategies; and finally, the evaluation stage—recording diagnostic and task-oriented evaluation metrics.

The entire framework adheres to three core design principles: (1) **Modularity**—each dimension can be completed independently; inapplicable fields can simply be marked as N/A with a brief explanation, without forcing researchers to fill in every field; (2) **Model-agnostic**—from classical Gaussian Processes to cutting-edge diffusion models, and from climate science to drug discovery, all model types and application domains can utilize the same reporting template; (3) **Reporting-driven**—researchers are not required to alter any existing methodological choices or workflows, but are merely asked to transparently document the choices made and their rationales. The recommended practice is to include a one-page SMRS card in the paper's appendix and link to an electronic version in the repository's README.

### Key Designs

The SMRS framework decomposes the surrogate modeling pipeline into six core reporting dimensions, each containing multiple levels of specific documentation elements to form a complete documentation chain from pre-modeling decisions to evaluation and validation.

**Dimension 1: Pre-assessment—Is a surrogate model genuinely needed?** This dimension requires researchers to seriously evaluate the necessity of a surrogate model before building it. The authors clearly define the intrinsic boundary of surrogate models: a surrogate model is an approximation of a "known but expensive generative process," which fundamentally differs from standard supervised learning—the latter learns to predict from observational data, rather than substituting a known process. The decision tree for determining the need for a surrogate model includes: if the performance bottleneck stems from inefficient code implementation, code-level optimizations (such as JIT compilation in JAX or type specialization in Julia) should be prioritized; if the problem is low-dimensional and computationally cheap, directly evaluating the original simulator might be more appropriate; if usable surrogate models already exist, the cost-effectiveness of reinventing the wheel needs evaluation; if the system is highly non-linear or exhibits discontinuities, surrogate models may fail to achieve acceptable approximation accuracy. Surrogate models are most suitable when: simulator evaluation is extremely expensive (e.g., hours to days for CFD simulations), the input-output mapping is sufficiently smooth to be approximated, and downstream tasks require massive repeated evaluations (e.g., thousands of evaluations in Bayesian optimization to find an optimal solution).

**Dimension 2: Data Collection and Generation.** This dimension requires detailed documentation across three major aspects. First is the classification and recording of **data source modes**: distinguishing between two types—(i) real-world data from physical experiments, sensor monitoring, clinical trials, etc., where the unique challenges lie in data quality, consistency, and completeness (measurement instrument bias and bias in sample collection protocols can propagate into the surrogate model); and (ii) computational data generated by numerical simulators or mechanistic models, further divided into offline mode (pre-running simulations in batches and storing them) and online mode (adaptively generating new simulation data based on the surrogate model's current uncertainty, particularly suited for active learning or Bayesian optimization workflows). Second is the selection and recording of **sampling design strategies**: ranging from simple random sampling and grid sampling (the former can leave large empty regions, while the latter suffers from the curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional spaces), to space-filling designs like Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and Sobol/Halton quasi-random sequences (achieving better spatial coverage with fewer points), to adaptive sampling methods such as active learning (selecting the most informative query points based on uncertainty), Bayesian optimization (efficiently balancing exploration and exploitation in low-dimensional spaces), meta-learning (rapidly adapting sampling strategies across related tasks), and reinforcement learning (training policy agents to learn how to propose input points that minimize surrogate model uncertainty). Third is the documentation of **data formats and metadata standards**: using domain-standard formats is recommended to enhance reusability and interoperability, including NetCDF for climate science, SMILES for molecular chemistry, HDF5 for engineering, Parquet for large-scale tabular data, and GeoTIFF for geographic raster data; metadata standards such as CF Conventions and Dublin Core document critical information like units, spatiotemporal resolution, variable definitions, and data provenance.

Additionally, the authors designed specific reporting guidelines for **multi-fidelity data** and **heterogeneous data sources**. Multi-fidelity surrogate models leverage a hierarchy of simulation fidelities from coarse to fine—using low-fidelity data for broad exploration and high-fidelity data for local refinement—thereby dramatically reducing the total simulation budget. SMRS requires documenting (i) which fidelity levels were used to train and evaluate the surrogate model, and (ii) how cross-fidelity information was integrated (e.g., co-kriging methods, transfer learning strategies, or hierarchical loss term designs). For heterogeneous data sources—where data comes from multiple simulators or measurement schemes with differences in scale, noise levels, or modeling assumptions, but do not form a clear fidelity hierarchy—SMRS introduces a **multi-source fidelity table** template: each data source lists its defining attributes (resolution, uncertainty level, governing equations, or instrument type), mapped to a shared "fidelity axis" (spatial, temporal, physical realism), and notes the comparability among data sources (comparable, partially comparable, or incomparable). When no meaningful hierarchical relationship exists, it is marked as N/A with a brief explanation.

**Dimension 3: Model Type Selection.** Four key attributes must be recorded: (1) **Deterministic vs. Probabilistic**—deterministic surrogates (standard feedforward networks, splines) return only single-point predictions and are trained by minimizing scalar losses, which is computationally efficient but fails to express prediction uncertainty; probabilistic surrogates (GP, BNN, deep generative models like diffusion models) output complete predictive distributions and are essential for handling stochastic simulators, parameter uncertainty, and uncertainty propagation tasks. Even if a deterministic model is chosen, uncertainty estimation can be obtained through post-hoc schemes like Deep Ensembles (training multiple independent models to obtain mean and variance) or MC-dropout (maintaining dropout during inference and performing multiple forward passes). SMRS requires reporting: whether prediction uncertainty is estimated, what method was used (ensembles, MC-dropout, variational inference, etc.), how uncertainty is utilized in the modeling pipeline, and whether sensitivity analysis was performed. (2) **Parametric vs. Non-parametric**—parametric models use a fixed parameter set and predefined functional forms, suitable for large-data, high-dimensional scenarios but limited by the inductive biases of the functional form; non-parametric models adapt their complexity based on data, suitable for small-data or poorly defined systems, but can be computationally prohibitive for large-scale problems. (3) **Conditionality**—many surrogate models are conditioned not only on inputs but also on auxiliary variables such as boundary conditions, environmental parameters, material properties, etc.; documenting the choice of conditional variables and their impact on generalization capability is crucial. (4) **Domain Constraint Integration**—physical laws (e.g., conservation of energy and mass), geometric constraints, and monotonicity requirements can be embedded through architectural design (such as PINNs adding differential equation residual terms to the loss function, or geometry-aware surrogate models) or training constraints.

**Dimension 4: Learning Methods.** This dimension covers the entire spectrum of learning paradigms from classical optimization to modern generative modeling. The authors categorize learning methods into the following classes and specify key info to report for each: (1) **Explicit Optimization**—first-order methods like gradient descent, Adam, RMSProp, second-order methods like quasi-Newton, or meta-heuristic methods (genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization) when the objective function is non-differentiable; (2) **Classical Bayesian Inference**—explicitly computing posteriors under conjugate settings (such as analytical GP posteriors), or using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) for posterior sampling, and using variational inference to convert posterior estimation into an optimization problem when sampling is too slow; (3) **Generalized Bayesian Inference**—replacing standard likelihood functions with task-specific losses (Bregman divergence, proper scoring rules) to achieve better robustness under model misspecification—a newer but crucial direction; (4) **Deep Generative Surrogates**—Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Normalizing Flows, diffusion models, and neural likelihood estimators, suitable for high-dimensional or multimodal outputs; (5) **Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)**—training generative model architectures on synthetic data when explicit likelihood is unavailable but the simulator is; (6) **Reinforcement Learning**—modeling adaptive sampling as a policy learning problem to select input points that maximize information gain or task rewards; (7) **AutoML**—automatically searching for optimal architectures and hyperparameters via Bayesian optimization or evolutionary strategies. For each method, SMRS requires reporting: specific optimization objectives or loss functions, the optimizers or samplers used, learning rate schedules and early stopping rules, prior distributions or scoring rules (Bayesian), simulation runs designed and data volume (SBI), and search space and evaluation metrics (AutoML).

**Dimension 5: Evaluation Metrics & Benchmarking.** SMRS categorizes evaluation into diagnostic and task-oriented evaluations. Diagnostic evaluation assesses the statistical properties of the surrogate model itself: (a) **Distributional Similarity**—quantifying the alignment between predicted distributions and true simulator output distributions using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Sliced Wasserstein Distance, etc.; (b) **Calibration**—Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC) verifies posterior consistency by sampling synthetic datasets from prior predictions, refitting the model to each dataset, and checking whether the true parameters are uniformly ranked in the recovered posterior (a well-calibrated model should yield a flat rank histogram); (c) **Generalization Performance Estimation**—using AIC and DIC in frequentist frameworks, and LOO-CV and WAIC in Bayesian frameworks. Task-oriented evaluation assesses the practical utility of surrogate models in downstream applications: using regret to measure the efficiency of finding optimal solutions in optimization, and utilizing coverage probability and prediction intervals to evaluate reliability in uncertainty quantification. SMRS also encourages authors to report stress testing results—at least one test probing model robustness (e.g., out-of-distribution generalization, long-horizon prediction, performance under noisy boundary conditions).

### Balancing Standardization and Innovation

Proposing a standardization framework inevitably invites skepticism regarding whether standardization will stifle innovation. The authors systematically discuss and address this in Section 3 from three dimensions:

The first concern is the **conflict between domain specificity and universal standards**. Different domains of surrogate modeling have vastly different requirements—climate science emphasizes rigorous uncertainty quantification and long-term prediction accuracy, aerospace engineering prioritizes real-time inference speed and physical constraint compliance, while drug discovery relies on molecular-specific SMILES string representations. SMRS responds to this with its core design principle of "prescribing evaluation dimensions rather than prescribing methods": each field in the schema defines the **type of information** to report (e.g., "what is the sampling design, and why was it chosen"), rather than specific methodological choices (e.g., "must use Latin Hypercube Sampling"). This means that a groundwater modeling study using MODFLOW to generate training data and a protein design study using molecular dynamics simulations can use the exact same SMRS template while entering domain-specific details.

The second concern is the **difficulty of integration with legacy systems and existing incentive structures**. Many fields rely on software tools developed decades ago (such as MODFLOW in hydrology, NetLogo for agent-based modeling) or use proprietary commercial software (such as ANSYS, COMSOL). Retrofitting these systems to accommodate new standards is technically difficult and conflicts with the academic incentive structure pushing for "fast publication." SMRS advocates progressive adoption: for workflows utilizing pre-generated simulation data, standard reporting can be integrated at the post-processing stage without modifying upstream simulators. Reviewers can use the SMRS checklist as a quick assessment tool to evaluate methodological transparency, creating top-down pressure for adoption.

The third concern is that **standardization might solidify current methods and inhibit frontier exploration**. Rapidly evolving directions like diffusion model surrogates and Transformer architectures may not yet have mature evaluation protocols and benchmarks. SMRS mitigates this concern by focusing on "documenting what was done and why" rather than "prescribing what should be done." A brand-new generative surrogate model method can use the SMRS template to fully document its design choices while entering any cutting-edge approach in the "Model Type" and "Learning Method" fields. The open benchmark and modular checklist design ensure that new methods can enter a comparable framework without being constrained by outdated standards.

## Key Experimental Results

### Literature Survey Coverage Analysis

This paper systematically reviewed 17 surrogate modeling papers spanning climate science, engineering optimization, epidemiology, drug discovery, materials science, and more. The review evaluated the reporting completeness and information extractability of each paper according to the six SMRS reporting dimensions. The following table synthesizes the reporting coverage across dimensions based on the authors' analysis:

| Reporting Dimension | Proportion of Fully Reporting Papers | Common Omissions | Impact on Reproducibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model Architecture Description | ~90% (15/17) | Occasional lack of hyperparameter details | Low—most architectures can be reproduced |
| Prediction Performance Metrics | ~85% (14/17) | Missing quantitative comparisons in a few papers | Medium—difficult to judge improvement without baselines |
| Data Collection Method | ~35% (6/17) | Quality control of data sources not specified | High—unable to judge data representativeness |
| Sampling Design Motivation | ~25% (4/17) | Rationale for choosing specific schemes not explained | High—unable to assess if data was "tailor-made" to favor the model |
| Uncertainty Quantification | ~30% (5/17) | UQ methods not reported or UQ omitted entirely | Extremely High—no UQ in high-risk scenarios is akin to "flying blind" |
| Limitations & Scope | ~20% (3/17) | Failure conditions and boundary cases not discussed | Extremely High—users do not know when to distrust the model |
| Reproducibility Info (Seeds/Code) | ~40% (7/17) | Random seeds, code paths, or hardware specifications missing | High—independent reproduction becomes a guessing game |

### Comparison of SMRS Dimension Mapping for Three Detailed Case Studies

The authors selected 3 papers with the richest reported information out of the 17 for in-depth SMRS mapping analysis, mapping all extractable information from each paper to fields in the SMRS report card template. The following table compares the coverage differences among the three case studies:

| SMRS Field | Case 1: Climate Surrogate | Case 2: Engineering Optimization Surrogate | Case 3: Epidemiological Surrogate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Justification for Requiring a Surrogate | Not explicitly discussed—modeling started directly | Briefly mentioned time-consuming CFD simulations without quantification | Not discussed—assumed readers knew computational costs of ABMs |
| Data Source and Mode | Detailed: NetCDF format climate simulation output, offline batch generation | Partially documented: simulation parameter ranges provided, but output formats omitted | Nicely documented: described configurations and run settings of the ABM simulator |
| Sampling Design and Motivation | LHS sampling with rationales provided (referencing space-filling design literature) | Grid sampling, no comparison with alternatives, why LHS wasn't used is unexplained | Uniform random sampling, no comparison with other schemes, no motivation explained |
| Model Type and Assumptions | GP (probabilistic, non-parametric), explicitly assumed stationary kernel | DNN (deterministic, parametric), why probabilistic models were avoided is unmentioned | Seq2Seq (conditional generation), reason for choosing conditional variables discussed |
| Uncertainty Quantification Method | GP posterior naturally provides—usage of predictive mean and variance documented | Entirely unreported—predictive confidence not discussed | Implemented via posterior sampling—calibration check results reported |
| Physical/Domain Constraint Integration | None (purely data-driven GP, climate physical constraints not embedded) | Yes (boundary conditions embedded in loss function) | Yes (mechanistic prior knowledge guided network architecture design) |
| Training Detail Completeness | Complete: reported optimizers, learning rates, kernel hyperparameter optimization | Partial: provided optimizer and learning rate, but missed early stopping and regularization details | Relatively complete: reported epochs, batch size, learning rate decay schedules |
| Diagnostic Evaluation Metrics | RMSE + Calibration test (posterior predictive interval coverage) | RMSE only—no distribution evaluation, no calibration check | RMSE + Coverage probability + Posterior rank histogram |
| Task-oriented Evaluation | Physical plausibility analysis of climate prediction uncertainty bands | Optimization regret curve—but not compared with simple baselines | Posterior quality of Bayesian inference and parameter recovery accuracy |
| Stress Testing/Robustness Analysis | Yes: tested prediction degradation under out-of-distribution climate scenarios | None: evaluated only within training distribution | None: used standard test set only |
| Code and Data Openness | Fully open: code repository + NetCDF dataset + random seeds | Partially open: code available, but complete dataset and seeds missing | Fully open: code + simulator settings + synthetic dataset |

### Key Findings

- **Reporting quality exhibits an extreme "inverted pyramid" distribution across dimensions**: Almost all papers extensively detailed model architecture and predictive accuracy (basic requirements for publication), whereas reporting rates plummeted to 20%-35% for similarly critical dimensions like sampling design motivation, uncertainty quantification, and limitation analysis. This finding quantitatively confirms a structural reporting bias in the surrogate modeling domain—publication incentives encourage "showing good results" rather than "transparently documenting the entire process."

- **The choice of probabilistic methods itself "forces" better UQ reporting**: Papers utilizing inherently probabilistic methods such as Gaussian Processes (e.g., Case 1) reported substantially higher UQ quality compared to those using deterministic DNNs (e.g., Case 2, which lacked UQ entirely). This pattern suggests that SMRS might need to impose clearer and stricter requirements on UQ reporting for deterministic models—such as forcing researchers to report whether uncertainty was considered and why they chose to omit UQ.

- **The high cost of cross-domain information extraction is vividly demonstrated by case studies**: Even when selecting the three most detailed papers, mapping them to the unified SMRS framework revealed numerous information gaps. The authors marked the difficulty of acquiring missing fields firsthand—for out-of-domain readers, positioning and understanding such information across highly specialized terminology is extremely time-consuming. An SMRS report card's structured table could reduce this process from hours to minutes.

- **The benchmarking ecosystem in surrogate modeling lags far behind mainstream ML subfields**: NLP has GLUE and SuperGLUE, while CV has ImageNet and COCO. However, surrogate modeling lacks cross-modal, version-controlled benchmark suites with unified evaluation protocols. While generic repositories like OpenML and UCI are helpful, they are not tailored to surrogate modeling scenarios and fail to cover unique evaluation demands like multi-fidelity, physical constraints, and out-of-distribution generalization.

## Highlights & Insights

- **The meta-design philosophy of "reporting rather than prescribing" is the most clever core concept of the paper.** The most common failure mode of standardization frameworks is "over-specification $\rightarrow$ stifling innovation $\rightarrow$ community resistance $\rightarrow$ standard abandonment." SMRS elegantly bypasses this deadlock by shifting the target of standardization from "methodological choice" to "information disclosure." Researchers remain free to choose any method, but SMRS requires them to transparently declare what they chose and why. This design pattern can be transferred to other fragmented fields in deep learning: Prompt Engineering reporting standards, data augmentation strategy reporting standards, model evaluation protocol reporting standards, etc.

- **Framing "is a surrogate model needed" as the first dimension reflects profound engineering judgment.** Many researchers introduce surrogate models by default under publication pressure, without evaluating simpler alternatives (code optimization, JIT compilation, reduced-order models, etc.). Formalizing this pre-assessment as the first step of the standard acts as a "necessity review gate" at the modeling pipeline entrance, systematically reducing superfluous surrogate model introduction and saving research resources.

- **The design of the multi-source fidelity table accommodates the complexity of real scientific workflows.** Textbook multi-fidelity modeling often assumes data sources form a clear low-to-high precision hierarchy. However, in reality, data often comes from different simulators, experimental protocols, or research groups, making simple accuracy ranking impossible. SMRS allows marking fields as "incomparable" with rationales, and this elegant compatibility with "messy reality" makes the framework genuinely practical.

- **The translation strategy from Model Cards to SMRS lowers the community adoption barrier.** Model Cards have already gained high awareness in industry and parts of academia. SMRS explicitly positions itself as a specialized adaptation of Model Cards for the surrogate modeling domain, leveraging existing conceptual foundations and community acceptance rather than demanding the community accept a brand-new standardization concept from scratch.

## Limitations & Future Work

- **The empirical efficacy of the framework lacks validation in real implementations**: The paper proposes the SMRS framework and demonstrates the need for it through literature reviews, but it has not tested the usability and adoption rate through the full pipeline of paper writing $\rightarrow$ submission $\rightarrow$ peer review $\rightarrow$ revision. A standard's success depends not only on design quality but also on community willingness to adopt and tool support. A prospective experiment could be designed—inviting several research groups to use the SMRS card in their next submission cycle and collecting feedback on their experience.

- **The lack of automated tool support poses a major barrier to practical adoption**: Currently, SMRS is positioned as a manually filled checklist. However, in the AI era, it is entirely feasible to develop automation tools: automatically extracting hyperparameters, optimizers, and loss functions from training code; parsing SMRS fields automatically from paper PDFs using LLMs; or even generating SMRS cards dynamically during model training via logging frameworks. The absence of such tools means researchers must invest extra time in writing documentation—directly conflicting with the "efficiency-seeking" culture of academia.

- **The sample size of 17 papers is small, limiting the statistical confidence of the conclusions**: While the case studies are illustrative, a total sample of 17 papers is insufficient to make reliable statistical inferences about the reporting state across the entire surrogate modeling field. Differences between subdomains (e.g., climate science reporting traditions might be significantly better than those in engineering optimization) could be obscured by averaging. Future work could scale to hundreds of papers, stratified by domain, year, and journal.

- **Under-coverage of ethical and regulatory dimensions in high-risk domains**: The SMRS framework lacks dedicated sections for ethical review, fairness assessment, or regulatory compliance. In areas like climate policy, clinical trials, and nuclear safety, merely reporting technical decisions may be insufficient—it is also necessary to record the potential social impacts of model decisions, differential impacts on specific subpopulations, and compliance with industry regulations.

- **Absence of planning for version control and community governance mechanisms**: Standards must evolve alongside technology—for instance, diffusion model surrogates barely existed two years ago, but are now a hot direction. The paper does not discuss version management strategies for SMRS (e.g., semantic versioning), community proposal and voting mechanisms (like RFC processes), and backward compatibility guarantees. The governance experiences of OpenAPI Specification and W3C standards could be leveraged.

## Related Work & Insights

- **vs Model Cards (Mitchell et al., 2019)**: Model Cards provide structured transparency documentation for general ML models, recording intended use, performance metrics, data bias, and applicability, and have been widely adopted by Google, Hugging Face, Microsoft, etc. SMRS inherits its concise "one-page card" format but is deeply customized for surrogate modeling—adding dimensions like sampling design, multi-fidelity data, physical constraint embedding, and simulation-based inference, which are not involved in standard ML. The relationship between the two is akin to "general reporting standards" vs "domain-specific reporting standards."

- **vs FAIR Principles (Wilkinson et al., 2016)**: FAIR focuses on findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability at the data level, serving as foundational principles in data management. SMRS extends the spirit of FAIR from data to complete modeling decisions—focusing not only on whether the data itself is FAIR but also on whether model selection, training strategies, and evaluation methods are transparently documented. The two are highly complementary; SMRS can integrate FAIR data citations as part of its data dimension.

- **vs SMT 2.0 (Saves et al., 2024)**: The Surrogate Modelling Toolbox is a standardized implementation of surrogate modeling at the engineering level, offering GP, mixed-variable models, etc. SMRS and SMT complement each other at different levels of abstraction—SMT standardizes "how to do" (code and algorithm levels), while SMRS standardizes "how to report what was done" (documentation and metadata levels). Ideally, SMT could build in functionality to automatically generate SMRS cards.

- **vs ClimateBench (Watson-Parris et al.)**: The climate domain has begun constructing dedicated benchmarks for surrogate models, but these benchmarks are highly domain-specific. SMRS provides a cross-domain meta-framework, allowing benchmarks in different fields such as climate, engineering, and biomedicine to reference and compare against each other on common reporting dimensions, thereby promoting the dissemination of cross-domain best practices.

- **Parallel Trends in LLM Transparency Reporting**: The Large Language Model domain similarly faces issues of opaque training data, inconsistent evaluation, and poor reproducibility. Meta's System Cards, Anthropic's Model Specs, and Google's AI Principles progress reports can be viewed as parallel implementations of the SMRS ideology in the LLM domain. SMRS's surrogate-model-specific design can provide reference experiences for customizing these generalized frameworks.

## Rating

- **Novelty**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Not an algorithmic innovation, but an important contribution at the methodological and standardization levels, pioneering in the surrogate modeling domain and accurately addressing the pain point of fragmentation.
- **Experimental Thoroughness**: ⭐⭐⭐ The 17-paper literature review and 3 case studies provide qualitative evidence, but it lacks large-scale quantitative statistics and prospective experimental validation of real-world adoption.
- **Writing Quality**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The paper is tightly structured and logically clear, with a highly balanced discussion of arguments and counter-arguments; the "alternative views" discussion in Section 3 is a model of academic writing.
- **Value**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Holds significant long-term infrastructure value for the surrogate modeling community, though its practical impact depends on community adoption rates, toolchain development, and policy support from journals and conferences.
