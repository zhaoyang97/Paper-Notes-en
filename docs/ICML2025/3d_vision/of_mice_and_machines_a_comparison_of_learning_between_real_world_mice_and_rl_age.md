# Of Mice and Machines: A Comparison of Learning Between Real World Mice and RL Agents

**Conference**: ICML2025  
**arXiv**: [2505.12204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12204)  
**Code**: TBD  
**Area**: Reinforcement Learning  
**Keywords**: Bio-inspired RL, Risk Aversion, Predator-Prey, Q-value Variance, Experience Replay

## TL;DR

This paper systematically compares the behavioral differences between real mice and RL agents in a predator-prey maze. Revealing that RL agents lack self-preservation instincts, the authors propose two mechanisms: Trauma-Inspired Safety Buffer (TISB) and Variance-Penalized TD learning (VP-TDMPC-2), which improve the state visitation overlap between agents and mice from 20.9% to 86.1%.

## Background & Motivation

RL has made significant progress in complex decision-making tasks recently, but a fundamental question remains open: **To what extent do the decision-making behaviors of RL agents truly capture the characteristics of biological organisms?**

The authors design a controlled comparative experiment—the "cellworld" hexagonal maze environment, where real mice must reach a goal location to obtain water rewards while evading a robotic predator. A corresponding Gymnasium simulation environment is constructed to control a virtual prey using RL algorithms. Through behavioral analysis, the authors identify several **key behavioral discrepancies**:

**Lack of self-preservation instinct**: RL agents often choose dangerous paths close to the predator for minor efficiency gains, whereas mice exhibit mature risk assessment behaviors.

**Thigmotaxis (wall-following)**: Approximately 70% of mice trajectories align with walls (an anxiety-related behavioral metric), whereas this rate is 0% for RL agents.

**Wait-and-evaluate behavior**: Mice stop and observe at the entrance when the predator is not visible, whereas RL agents cross directly.

**Differences in learning efficiency**: Mice develop persistent avoidance after only a single negative experience, whereas RL agents require multiple trials to adapt.

These observations form the core motivation of this paper: how to extract mechanisms from biological behaviors to induce more natural risk-aversive behaviors in RL agents.

## Method

### Experimental Environment

- **Physical Environment**: A hexagonal arena with 331 magnetic cells (11 cm spacing) with obstacles. Eight experimental mice (4 male, 4 female) are motivated by water restriction to explore. A high-speed tracking system (90Hz) records precise locations.
- **Robotic Predator**: Utilizes $A^*$ path planning + PID control. It tracks the last seen position when the mouse is visible, and moves randomly to blind spots when invisible.
- **Simulation Environment**: "cellworld gymnasium", replicating the physical layout and reward structure. Goal reached +1, captured -1. Partially observable: the agent detects the predator only within its line of sight.

### Baseline Method: TDMPC-2

The paper uses TDMPC-2 (model-based RL) as the baseline, which includes an encoder $z = h(s)$, latent dynamics $z' = d(z,a)$, reward predictor $r^* = R(z,a)$, value predictor $q^* = Q(z,a)$, and policy prior $\hat{a} = p(z)$. MPPI is used for action selection.

### Mechanism 1: Trauma-Inspired Safety Buffer (TISB)

Mimicking how biological systems learn from trauma, this mechanism consists of two components:

**(1) Threat Experience Rebalancing**: Ensures that each training batch contains a specified proportion of negative transitions. Experiments show that a **50% negative experience ratio** is optimal (the best performance across 5 seeds in a 5000-step experiment). This is analogous to how young animals develop risk assessment behaviors.

**(2) Threat Experience Amplification**: Amplifies the rewards of negative experiences by a factor of **200** during sampling, reflecting the emotional intensity amplification effect of trauma in biological systems. Smaller factors have limited effects, while factors greater than 200 slow down training.

TISB induces wider exploration and wall-following patterns in RL agents, but still lacks the entrance-waiting behavior characteristic of mice.

### Mechanism 2: Variance-Penalized TD learning (VP-TD)

**Why not SMiRL?** The authors initially attempted Surprise Minimization (SMiRL), adding the intrinsic reward $r_{\text{SMiRL}}(s_t) = -\log p(s_t | s_{<t})$ to TDMPC-2 and approximating the negative log-likelihood with latent space MSE. However, SMiRL **uniformly penalizes all state transitions**, which conversely hinders beneficial state transitions (e.g., from dangerous to safe) in partially observable environments and negates the path-diversifying effect of TISB.

**VP-TD Core Formulation**: Based on the observation that Q-value variance effectively indicates states that require cautious behavior, the authors propose modifying the TD target:

$$TD_{\text{target}} = r_t + \gamma \left( Q(s_{t+1}, \pi(s_{t+1})) - \alpha \text{Var}_{a \in A}(Q(s_{t+1}, a)) \right)$$

where $\alpha$ controls the strength of the variance penalty. During training, a grid is uniformly sampled from the action space, the Q-values of each action are calculated, and the variance is used as the penalty. **States where different actions lead to highly divergent outcomes (e.g., near the predator) receive larger penalties**.

**Comparison with SMiRL**:

| Metric / Dimension | SMiRL | VP-TD (Ours) |
|------|-------|---------------|
| Uncertainty Metric | State transition probability modeling | Q-value variance (directly measures decision uncertainty) |
| Penalty Execution | Statically penalizes all transitions via reward | Dynamically adjusted in TD target, context-dependent |
| Objective Function | $\max_\pi \mathbb{E}[\sum_t \gamma^t (r_t - \alpha_1 \log p(s_t \| s_{<t}))]$ | $\max_\pi \mathbb{E}[\sum_t \gamma^t (r_t - \alpha_2 \text{Var}_a(Q(s_t,a)))]$ |

## Main Results

### Behavioral Comparison Data

| Metric | TDMPC-2 | VP-TDMPC-2 | Real Mice |
|------|---------|------------|---------|
| Success Rate | ~80% | — | 86% |
| State Space Coverage | 21.3% | — | 77% |
| Overlap with Mice Visitation | **20.9%** | **86.1%** | 100% |
| Wait Behavior Ratio (1000 trajectories) | 0% | **32.4%** | 27.7% |
| Wall-following Trajectory Ratio | 0% | Partially improved | ~70% |
| Average Episode Steps | 7.09 | **13.89** | 14.04 |
| Distance Change before Predator Detection | -0.2 ~ -0.5 | -0.1 ~ -0.2 | -0.05 ~ -0.15 |

### Key Findings

1. **VP-TDMPC-2 increases the visitation pattern overlap rate from 20.9% to 86.1%**, which is the most central quantitative result.
2. The enhanced agent exhibits a **conservative motion pattern**, reducing the travel distance by approximately 45% upon initial entry into the environment, matching the cautious behavior of mice.
3. The wait behavior ratio (32.4%) is even slightly higher than that of real mice (27.7%), demonstrating that the variance penalty effectively induces hesitating/observing behavior.
4. The average episode steps (13.89 vs 14.04) match the mice almost perfectly.
5. **LLM agents (GPT)** also exhibit similar risky characteristics as traditional RL, lacking biologically aligned behaviors, suggesting this is a widespread issue across AI systems.

### TISB Ablation Study

- **Threat Experience Rebalancing**: A 50% negative experience ratio is optimal; higher or lower ratios both degrade performance.
- **Threat Experience Amplification**: An amplification factor of 200 is optimal, producing broader path diversity and wall-following behavior.
- TISB alone improves path diversity but is insufficient to generate wait behavior.

## Highlights & Insights

1. **Excellent interdisciplinary experimental design**: The precise alignment between real mouse experiments (8 mice, high-speed tracking, water restriction paradigm) and the simulation environment makes the comparisons convincing.
2. **Q-value variance as a risk signal** is a simple yet powerful insight—it directly utilizes the existing Q-network outputs without needing to model complex state transition probabilities.
3. **The improvement in overlap rate (86.1% vs 20.9%)** is an impressive result, with waiting behaviors and step counts matching those of mice almost perfectly.
4. The finding that LLMs also lack biologically aligned behavior has broad implications: current AI systems exhibit fundamental flaws in risk perception, regardless of their training paradigms.

## Limitations & Future Work

1. **Gap in wall-following behavior remains**: VP-TDMPC-2 improves coverage but fails to fully replicate the strong thigmotaxis of mice, with the central region still being used excessively.
2. **Environmental limitations**: Validated only in a single "cellworld" environment; generalizability remains unknown. More complex continuous spaces and multi-predator scenarios require further testing.
3. **Sensitivity to the variance penalty coefficient $\alpha$**: The paper does not analyze the robustness and optimal tuning strategies for $\alpha$ in detail.
4. **Biological correspondence**: The "200x trauma amplification" in TISB is an engineering choice rather than strict neuroscience modeling, leaving its biological plausibility questionable.
5. **Baseline restricted to TDMPC-2**: Whether the VP-TD concept can be transferred to other RL algorithms (such as SAC, PPO, etc.) remains unverified.
6. **Individual differences in mice**: Although 8 mice were used, analysis on individual variability regarding sex or temperament (bold vs. shy) is insufficient.

## Related Work & Insights

- **Cellworld Framework** (Lai et al., 2024): Provides a standardized experimental paradigm for predator-prey dynamics.
- **SMiRL** (Berseth et al., 2019): Surprise minimization RL; this paper demonstrates its limitations in partially observable environments.
- **PER** (Schaul et al., 2015): Prioritized Experience Replay accelerates learning but does not alter the behavioral policy.
- **Risk-Sensitive RL**: VP-TD in this paper can be viewed as a novel risk-sensitive objective function, complementing CVaR-based methods.
- **Insights**: The Q-value variance penalty concept can be extended to fields requiring cautious behavior, such as safe RL, robotic navigation, and autonomous driving.
