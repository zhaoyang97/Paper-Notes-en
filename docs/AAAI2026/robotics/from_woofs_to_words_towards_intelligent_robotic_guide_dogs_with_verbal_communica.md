# From Woofs to Words: Towards Intelligent Robotic Guide Dogs with Verbal Communication

**Conference**: AAAI 2026
**arXiv**: [2603.12574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12574)
**Code**: [Project Page](https://sites.google.com/view/woofs-words)
**Area**: Robotics
**Keywords**: Robotic Guide Dog, Dialogue System, LLM, Task Planning, Scene Verbalization

## TL;DR

This paper proposes a dialogue system for robotic guide dogs that leverages LLMs and a task planner to achieve **Plan Verbalization** and **Scene Verbalization**, supporting multi-turn natural language dialogue to assist visually impaired users in navigation decision-making. The system's effectiveness is validated through a real-user study and simulation experiments.

## Background & Motivation

### Real-World Need

Guide dogs are critical for the independent mobility of visually impaired individuals, yet breeding and training them takes years, is expensive, and yields graduation rates below 50%. Only approximately 2% of visually impaired Americans use guide dogs; in China, over 10 million visually impaired people are served by only around 400 guide dogs. Some individuals cannot keep guide dogs due to allergies or physical limitations. Robotic guide dogs therefore represent an alternative of significant social value.

### Limitations of Prior Work

- **Unidirectional communication systems**: Communicate solely via leash tension; the human fully follows the robot, assuming both parties know the destination in advance.
- **Limited bidirectional communication**: Interaction is restricted to buttons, predefined voice commands, or leash tugging.
- **Key Challenge**: No existing system supports natural multi-turn language dialogue—yet in real scenarios, guide dogs and their handlers must collaboratively decide on routes as a team.

### Two Novel Challenges

**Grounding open-vocabulary language to the environment**: When a user says "I'm thirsty," the system must map this ambiguous request to known navigable locations in the environment (e.g., water fountains, vending machines) and generate a feasible navigation plan.

**Spatial awareness for visually impaired users**: Contrary to common misconception, visually impaired users do not simply follow the guide dog—they make route decisions collaboratively as a team. The system must continuously describe the surrounding environment during navigation to help users maintain spatial awareness.

## Method

### Overall Architecture

The system comprises two core capabilities:

- **Plan Verbalization (pre-navigation)**: Presents candidate navigation plans generated by the task planner to the user in natural language, including information such as path cost and number of doors to open, to support informed user selection.
- **Scene Verbalization (during navigation)**: Converts real-time descriptions of the surrounding scene into speech during navigation execution, enhancing the user's spatial awareness.

The full pipeline runs as two concurrent threads: Thread 1 executes navigation actions; Thread 2 handles dialogue and scene verbalization.

### Key Designs

#### 1. **LLM-Based Multi-Turn Dialogue Management**

The system uses GPT-4 as the core dialogue engine. The LLM is configured as a guide dog persona, with a system prompt containing all valid navigable locations in the environment and the expected output format. When users issue ambiguous requests, the LLM clarifies intent through multi-turn dialogue.

**Safety guardrail mechanism**: Due to the non-deterministic nature of LLM outputs, a post-processing safety guard is incorporated. If the LLM fails to respond in the expected format or does not suggest any valid location, its response is replaced with a preset message: "I can only assist with navigating to nearby locations I know. Would you like to go somewhere nearby?"

#### 2. **Task Planner and Plan Verbalization**

The system uses an Answer Set Programming (ASP) planner to generate navigation plans. The planner operates over the following action set:

- `approach(door_id)` — approach a door
- `gothrough(door_id)` — pass through a door
- `opendoor(door_id)` — open a door
- `goto(location)` — navigate to a location

The planner generates optimal action sequences by minimizing total action cost (based on navigation distance plus additional cost for opening doors). ASP rules encode domain knowledge such as door accessibility relations.

**Core Idea**: The LLM "translates" the planner's output into natural language—e.g., "Going to the kitchen requires opening one door and takes about 3 minutes; going to the water fountain has no doors and takes about 1 minute. Where would you like to go?" This enables users to make informed decisions based on real constraints.

#### 3. **Scene Verbalization**

Once navigation begins, scene descriptions are triggered under the following conditions:
- The robot's position crosses a semantic region boundary (e.g., transitioning from "corridor" to "kitchen")
- A prolonged silence occurs during dialogue

Example broadcasts: "We are navigating down a long corridor… We are approaching an office door… We have arrived at the office door."

### Loss & Training

No training is required. Speech-to-text is handled by the Vosk model, dialogue is driven by GPT-4, and planning is performed by an ASP solver. The overall framework is built on ROS and deployed on the Unitree Go2 quadruped robot platform.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results: Real-User Study

Seven legally blind participants (5 male, 2 female, ages 40–68) navigated from an entrance to a conference room in a real indoor environment. A within-subjects design was employed across three verbalization conditions:

| Condition | Q1 Usefulness | Q2 Helpfulness | Q3 Comparative Help | Q4 Safety | Q5 Ease of Communication | Q6 Preference Shift |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|--------------------|-----------|--------------------------|--------------------|
| Minimal Verbalization | 4.33 | 4.67 | 3.50 | 4.00 | 4.33 | 3.17 |
| Scene Verbalization Only | 4.67 | 4.83 | 4.00 | 4.00 | 4.00 | 3.17 |
| **Ours (Scene + Plan)** | **4.83** | **4.83** | **4.33** | 3.83 | **4.50** | **3.67** |

- The proposed method achieved the highest scores on interface usefulness (Q1), ease of communication (Q5), and preference relative to a real guide dog (Q6).
- The safety rating (Q4 = 3.83) was slightly lower; qualitative feedback suggests this may reflect discomfort from first-time interaction with a robot.

### Simulation Experiments: Accuracy and Efficiency

GPT-4 was used to simulate visually impaired users across 77 ⟨location, intent⟩ pairs:

| Method | Accuracy | Avg. Dialogue Turns |
|--------|----------|---------------------|
| Keyword Matching | ~75% | ~5 (verbose) |
| Single-Turn LLM | ~85% | 1 |
| **Ours** | **94.8%** | **~3** |

### Ablation Study: Robustness and Planning Information

**Noise robustness** (character perturbation with probability 0.3):

| Method | Accuracy (No Noise) | Accuracy (With Noise) | Degradation |
|--------|--------------------|-----------------------|-------------|
| Keyword Matching | ~75% | ~15% | Catastrophic |
| **Ours** | 94.8% | 89.6% | Only 5.2% |

**Effect of planning information**:

| Configuration | Dialogue Length | Navigation Cost | Total Time |
|---------------|----------------|-----------------|------------|
| With planning info | 115.67 | 55.17 | **230.15** |
| Without planning info | 84.83 | 73.00 | 277.27 |

Including planning information increases dialogue length but reduces navigation cost, yielding approximately 17% reduction in total time.

### Key Findings

1. Combined plan and scene verbalization is the most preferred communication strategy among users.
2. The LLM-based multi-turn dialogue system is highly robust to speech recognition noise, whereas keyword matching completely fails under noisy conditions.
3. Providing users with planning-level information (distance, number of doors, etc.) significantly assists them in selecting more efficient routes.

## Highlights & Insights

- **Deep integration of NLP/LLM capabilities with classical symbolic planning**: The LLM handles intent understanding and natural language generation, while the ASP planner handles precise spatial reasoning—a complementary design that compensates for LLM weaknesses in spatial inference.
- **Commendable commitment to real-user HRI research**: Recruiting legally blind participants rather than simulated users lends greater validity to the findings.
- **Safety guardrail design**: Format checking of LLM outputs and replacement of invalid responses reflect a strong emphasis on system safety.

## Limitations & Future Work

- The human study sample size is very small (only 6 valid participants), limiting the statistical power of quantitative conclusions.
- The real-user experiment employed Wizard of Oz teleoperation for robot motion, leaving fully autonomous navigation unvalidated.
- The scene verbalization strategy is relatively simple (triggered by semantic region boundary crossings); learned strategies are not explored.
- Outdoor scenarios, dynamic obstacles, and robot fall recovery are not addressed.

## Related Work & Insights

- Compared to existing robotic guide systems such as Kim et al. (2023) and DeFazio et al. (2023), this work is the first to realize natural multi-turn language dialogue for guide robot navigation.
- The ASP planner could be replaced by LLM-based task planning approaches (e.g., SayCan), though symbolic planners retain advantages in precise spatial reasoning.
- Insight: Dialogue system design for guide robot scenarios must carefully attend to information density and presentation—too much information may distract the user, while too little fails to support decision-making.

## Rating

- Novelty: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ — First work to integrate LLM dialogue and task planning for verbalized interaction with a robotic guide dog.
- Experimental Thoroughness: ⭐⭐⭐ — The real-user study is valuable but limited by small sample size; simulation experiments are well-designed.
- Writing Quality: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ — Motivation is clearly articulated and system description is complete.
- Value: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ — Carries significant social importance for assistive robotics and accessibility for visually impaired individuals.
