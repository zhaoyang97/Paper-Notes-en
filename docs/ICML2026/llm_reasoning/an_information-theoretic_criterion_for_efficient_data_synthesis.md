# An Information-Theoretic Criterion for Efficient Data Synthesis

**Conference**: ICML2026  
**arXiv**: [2605.16379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16379)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: LLM Pre-training  
**Keywords**: Synthetic Data, Information Theory, Data Processing Inequality, External Verifiers, Reward Hacking  

## TL;DR
This paper uses the Data Processing Inequality to explain why synthetic data is sometimes effective and sometimes leads to model collapse: synthetic data is information-open only when the training closed-loop continuously introduces stable external signals; high meta-level verification signals are more efficient and generalizable than instance-level imitation.

## Background & Motivation
**Background**: LLM training increasingly relies on synthetic data. High-quality real text is approaching a supply bottleneck, while tasks like mathematics, code, tool use, and long-range reasoning require stronger supervision signals than ordinary web corpora. Successful cases include verifier-guided synthesis, RLVR, program test feedback, formal proof checkers, and fixed rubric evaluators.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Synthetic data is a double-edged sword. Repeated self-training using the model's own outputs often causes distribution shrinkage, loss of long-tail patterns, and performance degradation. However, synthetic pipelines with verifiers or environment feedback can yield significant gains. While many empirical observations exist, a unified explanation is lacking: when does synthetic data inject new information, and when does it merely cycle through the model's existing distribution?

**Key Challenge**: Data generated by the model itself does not increase information about the real task out of thin air. If training samples come entirely from the current model without feedback independent of the model's state, the Data Processing Inequality (DPI) implies that task-relevant information will only remain constant or decrease. Paradoxically, implementations like RLVR, code testing, and proof checking drive progress, suggesting successful pipelines are not closed systems.

**Goal**: The authors aim to provide an information-theoretic criterion to determine the effectiveness of synthetic data pipelines and explain why certain external signals generate cross-domain generalization with fewer samples while others provide limited gains despite large data volumes.

**Key Insight**: The paper formulates the training process through relationships between random variables: the real task structure $X$, existing data $D$, model state $Z$, and external signals $S$. If $X \to D \to Z$ or $X \to Z_t \to D_t^{syn} \to Z_{t+1}$ forms an information closed-loop, DPI results in monotonically non-increasing information. By introducing a stable $S$, the upper bound becomes $I(X;D,S)$ or $I(X;Z_t,S)$.

**Core Idea**: The efficacy of synthetic data depends not on whether it is "model-generated," but on whether the training loop is information-open and whether external signals inject task-relevant information at a high meta-level.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
This paper does not propose a new training algorithm but provides a set of information-theoretic "judgment criteria" for synthetic data pipelines. The core approach involves defining the training process through the relationships between random variables—task structure $X$, data $D$, model state $Z$, and external signals $S$—to analyze how the Data Processing Inequality (DPI) allows information to flow. It distinguishes between information-closed and information-open pipelines to explain model collapse, uses "meta-level" to characterize sample efficiency differences across SFT, RFT, and RLVR, and employs task-relevant partitions to decompose supervision signals into effective task information and intra-class noise, unifying generalization, diversity, and reward hacking within a single framework.

Training is abstracted as mapping data $D$ and internal randomness $R$ to the model state $Z = f_{train}(D,R)$. Without additional side information about the task structure $X$, a Markov chain $X \to D \to Z$ exists, and DPI gives $I(X;Z) \leq I(X;D)$—closed-loop self-training cannot systematically increase the model's information regarding the real task. For iterative synthetic data, if $D_t^{syn}$ is generated solely by $Z_t$ without verifiers, environments, or new data, then $X \to Z_t \to D_t^{syn} \to Z_{t+1}$ implies $I(X;Z_{t+1}) \leq I(X;Z_t)$. Actual degradation (sampling error, optimization error, distribution shrinkage) further tightens this inequality. The pivot occurs with the introduction of an external signal $S$, where the upper bound relaxes to $I(X;Z) \leq I(X;D,S) = I(X;D) + I(X;S \mid D)$. All truly new information originates from the conditional mutual information $I(X;S \mid D)$.

### Key Designs

**1. Information-open criterion: Determining sustainable gains using conditional mutual information**

Synthetic data is a double-edged sword—pure self-training collapses while verifier-based pipelines thrive, but a unified criterion was previously missing. This paper provides a clear standard: as long as the external signal $S$ satisfies $I(X;S \mid D) > 0$ (or $I(X;S \mid Z_t) > 0$ in iterative cases), it carries task-relevant information beyond the current model/data, making the pipeline information-open. Conversely, without such $S$, random sampling provides diverse candidates but lacks the direction to select those closer to the real task, closing the loop and allowing DPI to reduce information. This explains why closed-loop self-training inevitably collapses and why code tests, proof checks, and fixed rubrics are effective—they are stable external signals that do not drift with the student model.

**2. Meta-level information injection: Signal level determines sample efficiency**

Being information-open is insufficient; sample efficiency varies across effective pipelines due to the "level" of signal injection. High meta-level signals only distinguish behaviorally important equivalence classes (e.g., "is the answer correct?"), while low meta-level signals require replicating a specific reference answer. If a problem has $M$ equally acceptable answers, high-level signals treat them as one class, whereas instance-level SFT spends up to $\log M$ bits identifying a specific surface form, which is valueless for the task. This explains why binary correctness in RLVR generalizes across math and code (restricting only "what is right"), while single-reference imitation wastes capacity on irrelevant phrasing and formatting details.

**3. Decomposition of supervision information: An information-theoretic explanation for reward hacking**

Given a task-relevant partition $\pi$ (grouping outputs by "task equivalence"), any supervision signal satisfies the decomposition:

$$I(Y;S \mid Q) = I([Y]_\pi; S \mid Q) + I(Y; S \mid [Y]_\pi, Q)$$

The first term $I([Y]_\pi; S \mid Q)$ represents the true task-relevant gain, while the second is the intra-class gain (surface differences within the same equivalence class). Efficiency $\eta_\pi$ is defined as the proportion of the task-relevant term; when the signal depends only on $[Y]_\pi$, $\eta_\pi = 1$. This clarifies reward hacking: if a coarser, easier-to-learn proxy signal (e.g., length, style) correlates with the reward, the model's simplicity bias in gradient learning will prioritize this proxy partition. Consequently, training rewards rise while true accuracy stagnates. Reward hacking is thus reframed as the model learning the most information-efficient components of the training signal.

### Loss & Training
The paper compares three signal injection methods within this framework: SFT maximizes log-likelihood for fixed pairs (raising a specific answer's probability); RFT filters model-generated candidates via an acceptance test followed by SFT; RLVR incorporates verifier rewards directly into policy gradients using advantage weighting. The fundamental divide is not whether the data is synthetic, but how the external signal enters the gradient and sample selection, corresponding to the information-open and meta-level dimensions.

This paper focuses on theoretical and case analysis rather than traditional benchmarks. Main results are summarized through criteria and case evidence.

### Ablation Study
The analytical ablations focus on "externality," "signal granularity," and "removal of spurious correlations."

### Key Findings
- The bottleneck of synthetic data is verification capacity. Progress in math and code is faster because these domains provide stable, high meta-level external signals.
- Randomness and external signals are both essential. Sampling provides diversity, while signals provide direction; sampling alone leads to collapse, and signals alone offer no exploration.
- The reason diversity outperforms repetition can be explained by partition coverage: repeated prompt-output pairs offer almost no new task information, while samples covering new partition blocks provide fresh bits.

## Highlights & Insights
- The paper elevates "synthetic data effectiveness" from an empirical recipe to an information boundary problem. It forces the question: which variable in the pipeline carries task information beyond the current model?
- The "meta-level" concept is highly explanatory. Successes like unit tests, Lean proof checkers, and format rewards all compress diverse outputs into behavioral equivalence classes.
- The explanation for reward hacking is actionable: if a proxy feature is more information-efficient than the real target, learning it is a rational optimization outcome. Data construction must actively decouple length, style, and source models.
- Training design insight: instead of pursuing more synthetic samples, invest in stable verifiers, fixed judges, executable environments, and diverse prompt coverage.

## Limitations & Future Work
- The framework is primarily qualitative and does not predict specific sample counts or required verifier accuracy for a pipeline to succeed.
- The thesis that "models prioritize higher information-efficiency signals" relies on simplicity bias and case support rather than a strict derivation from mutual information decomposition.
- External signals are treated as given, but real-world verifiers have errors and vulnerabilities; designing verifiers that remain robust as model capability increases is an open problem.
- For open-ended generation and creative tasks, high meta-level signals are hard to construct, likely requiring a mix of human labels, fixed rubrics, and behavioral tests.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs Model Collapse Research**: While previous work observed degradation in self-consuming models, this paper explains it as an inevitable trend of information-closed loops.
- **vs RLVR / Verifiable Reward**: The success of RLVR is not just about RL techniques but about the verifier acting as an external signal that keeps the loop information-open.
- **vs SFT Synthetic Data**: SFT efficiently distills fixed data, but wastes capacity on surface forms when multiple answers are acceptable; this is identified as the inefficiency of low meta-level signals.
- **vs Sutton's Bitter Lesson**: Letting "computation discover structure" is interpreted as using meta-level constraints rather than hard-coding instance-level knowledge.
