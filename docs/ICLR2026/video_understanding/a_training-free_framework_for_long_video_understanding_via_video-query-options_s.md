# A Training-Free Framework for Long Video Understanding via Video-Query-Options Similarity

**Conference**: ICLR2026  
**OpenReview**: [https://openreview.net/forum?id=hfMfYMoRLk](https://openreview.net/forum?id=hfMfYMoRLk)  
**Code**: https://github.com/wuzhirong520/VTR-VLM  
**Area**: Video Understanding  
**Keywords**: Long Video Understanding, Training-Free, Keyframe Retrieval, Adaptive Sampling, Multi-modal Large Language Model

## TL;DR
Addressing the issue that hour-long videos cannot fit into the context window of Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs), this paper proposes a **training-free** input-side framework. It utilizes a video-text retrieval model to score the relevance of video segments, followed by **Adaptive Frame Sampling** (AFS) and **Dynamic Resolution Allocation** (DRA). The relevance estimation is refined by incorporating candidate answers generated by the MLLM itself into the retrieval query (VQOS). This framework achieves an average improvement of 3~5 points for LLaVA-Video and Qwen2.5-VL across five long video benchmarks.

## Background & Motivation
**Background**: MLLMs have demonstrated strong performance on images and short videos but generally fail when encountering hour-long videos. The fundamental bottleneck is that the **limited context window cannot accommodate massive spatio-temporal content**. For instance, a 67-minute video sampled at 1 fps yields thousands of frames, far exceeding the token budget. Current solutions follow two paths: **context expansion** (e.g., LongVILA using multi-stage training and parallel techniques to extend sequence length) and **token compression** (e.g., hierarchical compression in InternVideo2.5 or task-aware KV sparsification in VideoXL-2), both of which attempt to pack more information into a fixed budget.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Both paths require **expensive training**. Given the rapid iteration of MLLM architectures, a compressor trained for a specific backbone today may need retraining when the backbone changes, leading to high adaptation costs. Consequently, training-free approaches are emerging, with **retrieval-based** methods being the most suitable—utilizing the image-text retrieval capabilities of models like CLIP/SigLIP to select keyframes semantically relevant to the question for MLLM processing. However, existing retrieval-based methods have two major flaws: (1) most perform only **static image-text retrieval**, ignoring temporal dynamics, causality, and actions (e.g., Figure 1(b): for the question "What did Dominique do after the man in red came on stage?", a single frame cannot distinguish "hugging" from "slapping"); (2) they **can select but cannot refine**—once keyframes are chosen, the information within them is neither deeply explored nor optimally utilized.

**Key Challenge**: Under a fixed token budget, there is a direct trade-off between **frame count and single-frame resolution**. Increasing the frame count requires shrinking each frame, resulting in the loss of spatial details. Increasing clarity restricts the number of frames, leading to insufficient temporal coverage. Existing methods treat all segments equally for sampling and scaling, failing to tilt the precious budget toward truly relevant content.

**Key Insight**: The authors imitate the human cognitive process for answering questions about long videos: **Hypothesis Generation → Focused Verification → Irrelevance Filtering**. This provides three levers: utilizing stronger video-text retrieval models to calculate relevance, densely sampling high-relevance segments at higher resolutions, and allowing the model to generate hypotheses to strengthen retrieval.

**Core Idea**: Using video-text retrieval relevance scores as a "conductor," the framework performs adaptive frame sampling and resolution allocation on the **input side**, refining these scores with candidate options generated by the MLLM—all without updating model weights.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
The input consists of a long video and a user question, while the output is the answer. The entire pipeline occurs **before** feeding frames to the MLLM and does not touch internal weights, making it naturally plug-and-play.

The workflow is: the long video is uniformly cut into $m$ equal-length segments (set to 16 seconds in implementation); a pre-trained Video-Text Retrieval (VTR) model, PE-G/14, calculates the cosine similarity between each segment and the query to obtain initial scores $S^0$. To refine relevance estimation, the MLLM briefly observes a coarsely sampled version of the video to **generate several candidate answer options**. The question is concatenated with each option to form multiple text strings for retrieval, with the maximum similarity used as the refined score $S$ (VQOS). Guided by this score, **Adaptive Frame Sampling (AFS)** assigns more frames to high-relevance segments, and **Dynamic Resolution Allocation (DRA)** assigns higher resolutions to high-relevance frames. Finally, the carefully selected frames are fed into the MLLM alongside the question. AFS and DRA share the same similarity scores, representing budget allocation across two orthogonal dimensions: "how many frames" and "how clear each frame is."

### Key Designs

**1. Video-Query-Options Similarity (VQOS): Guessing before Retrieving**

This is the core innovation, addressing the pain points of "static retrieval failing temporal questions" and "inaccurate relevance estimation." Given segment $V_i$ and query $Q$, the VTR model extracts video features $f_{v_i}$ and text features $f_q$ to compute initial cosine similarity $S^0_i = \frac{f_{v_i}\cdot f_q}{\|f_{v_i}\|\cdot\|f_q\|}$. However, using the question alone provides limited information; questions are often open-ended/weakly aligned with segment semantics. Thus, the MLLM generates $z$ **candidate answer options** based on a coarse view of the video. The question is combined with each option to form $z$ statements $T=\{T_1,\dots,T_z\}$ (e.g., "What does she do..." becomes "...she hugs him"). These are encoded into features $F$, and the final score is the **maximum similarity across all text features**:

$$S_i = \max_{f\in F}\frac{f_{v_i}\cdot f}{\|f_{v_i}\|\cdot\|f\|}.$$

**Mechanism**: A complete "question + hypothesis" statement is semantically richer and more specifically aligned with video segments than a standalone question—mimicking the human process of verifying hypotheses. Taking the maximum ensures that if any hypothesis hits a segment, the segment is deemed highly relevant.

**2. Adaptive Frame Sampling (AFS): Tilting Frame Budgets toward Relevance**

To avoid the waste of uniform sampling, $N$ frames are sampled by selecting top-$k$ segments. The goal is to allocate $p_i$ frames per segment such that $\sum_{i=1}^k p_i = N$ and $S_i \le S_j \Rightarrow p_i \le p_j$. The authors use a layered approximation: top-$k$ segments are sorted by similarity and divided into $L_1$ sampling levels. Level $l$ contains $m_l$ segments, each sampled at $c_l$ frames, satisfying $\sum_l m_l = k$, $\sum_l m_l c_l = N$, and $c_1>c_2>\dots>c_{L_1}$. Using predefined sampling rates $\{c_l\}$ (e.g., $\{8,16,32\}$), the number of segments per level $\{m_l\}$ is solved via the PuLP linear programming tool.

**3. Dynamic Resolution Allocation (DRA): Tilting Resolution Budgets**

Addressing the "frame count ↔ resolution" trade-off. For a fixed token budget $P$, keyframes receive high resolution while non-key frames receive low resolution. $L_2$ resolution levels are defined with scaling factors $\alpha_i \in(0,1]$, maintaining aspect ratios: $H_i=\lfloor\frac{\alpha_i H}{I}\rfloor\cdot I$, where $I=28$ is the patch size constraint. Allocation $\{n_i\}$ satisfies $\sum_i n_i = N$ and $\sum_i n_i H_i W_i = P$. Top similarity frames are assigned to $(H_1, W_1)$. To simplify, the budget is split equally among resolution levels, so $\hat n_i=\lfloor\frac{P}{L\cdot H_i W_i}\rfloor$. DRA is only enabled for backbones supporting variable resolution (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL).

**4. Mechanism: Algorithm 1 Scheduling**

A pipeline that serializes these components. First, $S^0$ is calculated to select a candidate pool $V'$ of top-$R\cdot N$ segments (coarse filtering). Then, $R$ rounds of option generation occur: segments are sampled from $V'$ using $S^0$-guided AFS+DRA and fed to the MLLM. All generated options are accumulated. Finally, refined scores $S$ (VQOS) are computed using the "question + options" set to select the final frames via AFS+DRA for the conclusive answer. Rounds of option generation are **temporally independent and can be parallelized**.

### Loss & Training
Ours is training-free; no parameter updates or fine-tuning are involved. Key hyperparameters: 16s segments, PE-G/14 for VTR, 1 fps. LLaVA-Video: 64 frames, top-16 segments, levels $\{2,4,8\}$, no DRA. Qwen2.5-VL: 768 frames, top-48 segments, levels $\{8,16,32\}$, token budget $20480\times 28 \times 28$.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results
Evaluated on 5 benchmarks (LVBench 67min, MLVU 13min, LongVideoBench 8min, VideoMME 17min, VideoEval-Pro 38min) using LLaVA-Video and Qwen2.5-VL (7B/72B). **Ours-GO** denotes self-generated options; **Ours-PO** denotes using provided multiple-choice options (upper bound).

|--------|------|------|------|------|------|------|
| LLaVA-Video | 42.0 | 69.3 | 57.4 | 63.2 | — | 50.6 |
| + AKS | 47.0 | 69.1 | 62.9 | 65.3 | — | 54.1 |
| + AdaReTake | 49.6 | 70.6 | 59.6 | 64.0 | — | 54.2 |

At the 7B scale, Ours-GO improves LLaVA-Video / Qwen2.5-VL by **5.3% / 5.0%** on average. The advantage is more pronounced on longer benchmarks (LVBench 67min, VideoEval-Pro 38min), where the 7B models outperform baselines by **8.5% / 8.3%**. Since AdaReTake is a token-level compressor and this method is input-level, combining them (**Ours-GO + AdaReTake**) yields an additional ~1% gain.

### Key Findings
- **DRA is the most significant component for Qwen2.5-VL (+2.1%)**, but inapplicable to fixed-resolution models like LLaVA-Video—indicating that resolution budget allocation value depends on backbone flexibility.
- **Segment-level uniform sampling yields negligible gains (+0.2%/+0.0%)**; the real boost comes from AFS "dense sampling" and VQOS "hypothesis-enhanced retrieval."
- **Longer videos yield higher gains** (+8.5% on LVBench), confirming that relevance-guided allocation is more valuable as redundancy increases.

## Highlights & Insights
- **Turning "generated hypotheses" into retrieval queries (VQOS) is clever**: Questions alone are often open-ended; adding a specific hypothesis increases information density. The "max" strategy recognizes that "if any hypothesis hits a segment, the segment is relevant."
- **AFS and DRA solve budget allocation across "frame count" and "resolution" dimensions**, offering a much finer-grained approach than "one-size-fits-all uniform sampling."
- **Zero-training and input-side nature** makes the method orthogonal to token-level compressors (AdaReTake) and easily transferable to new backbones—addressing the high adaptation cost of trained compressors in a rapidly evolving field.

## Limitations & Future Work
- **Reliance on a strong VTR model**: PE-G/14 is accurate but slow (15.5s per minute of video). While this can be hidden in streaming scenarios, it remains a cost in batch processing.
- **Option quality**: Ours-GO depends on the MLLM's initial coarse understanding of the video; poor initial observations lead to low-quality hypotheses.
- **Empirical Hyperparameters**: Values for segment length and sampling levels were manually tuned; there is room for automated, adaptive selection.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. Context Expansion (LongVILA / VideoXL-2)**: These require expensive training and are backbone-specific; this method is training-free and plug-and-play.
- **vs. Agent-based methods (Deep Video Discovery)**: Agent-based methods rely on heavy computation for video descriptions (losing visual details); this method works directly on pixels/frames.
- **vs. Retrieval methods (AKS / AdaReTake)**: AKS uses static CLIP features and fails on temporal questions. This method uses **video-text** retrieval + VQOS for refined estimation and applies it to both sampling and resolution.
