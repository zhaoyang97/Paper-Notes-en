# Bias in the Mirror: Are LLMs' Opinions Robust to Their Own Adversarial Attacks

**Conference**: ACL 2025  
**arXiv**: [2410.13517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13517)  
**Code**: None  
**Area**: AI Safety  
**Keywords**: LLM bias, self-debate, adversarial attacks, opinion robustness, multilingual

## TL;DR
This paper proposes a novel "self-debate" paradigm where two instances of the same LLM play the proponent and opponent to debate each other, attempting to persuade a neutral version of the model. This setup is used to evaluate the robustness of LLMs' intrinsic bias—specifically, whether the bias is easily swayed and whether the model is susceptible to being misled by its own adversarial arguments.

## Background & Motivation

**Background**: Numerous studies have revealed various biases in LLMs—such as political bias, gender bias, and cultural bias. These biases typically stem from the training data and alignment processes. Existing work primarily focuses on bias detection and measurement, evaluating biased tendencies of models through surveys, political compass tests, etc.

**Limitations of Prior Work**: Most prior studies detect bias in static scenarios—presenting a question to a model and observing its stance in the output. However, real-world LLM deployment is dynamically interactive, and biases can be reinforced or altered during conversations. Crucially, there is a lack of understanding regarding bias "robustness": is a model's bias deeply ingrained or easily persuaded to change?

**Key Challenge**: A bias that is too "resolute" implies that the model is stubborn and cannot be corrected; a bias that is too "fragile" implies that the model is easily manipulated to reinforce misinformation. Both extremes are problematic, yet the community does not even know which is the actual case.

**Goal**: To quantitatively evaluate the robustness of LLM biases through self-debate experiments, studying the persistence and mutability of model bias when faced with its own adversarial arguments.

**Key Insight**: Letting the LLM debate with itself. Since the model best understands its own "persuasive methods," adversarial arguments generated by itself might serve as the strongest test of bias.

**Core Idea**: Construct a "self-debate" experimental paradigm—using three instances of the same model (proponent, opponent, and judge) to test the robustness of LLM biases using the LLM's own argumentation capabilities.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
The experiment is designed as follows: For a controversial topic (e.g., abortion rights, gun control, etc.), the LLM's initial stance is first queried. Then, two instances of the LLM are configured—one instructed to hold a pro-stance, and the other a con-stance—to conduct multiple rounds of debate. After the debate concludes, a "neutral" LLM instance that did not participate in the debate reads the debate history and generates its own final decision. The system evaluates whether the neutral model's stance changed under the influence of the arguments in the debate.

### Key Designs

1. **Three-Role Self-Debate Architecture**:

    - **Function**: Create a model self-confrontation environment to test bias robustness.
    - **Mechanism**: The same LLM (such as GPT-4, LLaMA, etc.) is instantiated into three roles: Advocate (instructed to support a stance), Opponent (instructed to oppose the stance), and Judge (remains neutral, reads the arguments of both sides, and makes a judgment). Advocate and Opponent conduct multiple alternating rounds of debate, with each round generating structured arguments (claims + evidence + rebuttals). After the debate is completed, the Judge is required to express a stance based on the quality of arguments and logical strength of both sides.
    - **Design Motivation**: Using different instances of the same model ensures that both debating sides share the same knowledge base and linguistic capacity, so that the experiment truly tests the "robustness of bias" rather than "differences in reasoning capabilities."

2. **Multi-dimensional Bias Evaluation Metrics**:

    - **Function**: Comprehensively quantify the changes in bias before and after the debate.
    - **Mechanism**: Design four evaluation dimensions: (a) stance consistency—whether the Judge's final stance is consistent with the model's initial bias; (b) persuasion rate—the percentage of trials where the Judge is successfully persuaded to change its stance; (c) change in bias intensity—measuring the difference in bias intensity before and after using a Likert scale; (d) asymmetric response—whether the model's response levels to pro and con arguments are symmetric.
    - **Design Motivation**: A single metric cannot capture the full picture of bias robustness. Stances may remain unchanged while confidence declines, or stances may change but only on specific topics—requiring analysis from multiple angles.

3. **Cross-Model, Cross-Lingual, and Cross-Topic Experimental Design**:

    - **Function**: Evaluate the performance of bias robustness under different conditions.
    - **Mechanism**: Conduct experiments across multiple LLMs (models of different scales and sources like GPT-4, GPT-3.5, LLaMA-2, etc.), multiple languages (English, French, etc.), and multiple controversial topics, and analyze whether bias robustness exhibits a consistent pattern.
    - **Design Motivation**: Bias may perform differently under diverse language and cultural contexts—for example, a French model may exhibit different bias patterns on social issues compared to an English model.

### Loss & Training
This paper is an evaluation study and does not involve model training. The experiments control the behavior of different roles through prompt engineering.

## Key Experimental Results

### Main Results

| Model | Initial Bias Consistency Rate | Post-Debate Stance Retention Rate | Persuaded to Change Rate | Change in Bias Intensity |
|------|---------------|-----------------|-------------|-------------|
| GPT-4 | 78% | 62% | 38% | -0.8 |
| GPT-3.5 | 72% | 48% | 52% | -1.2 |
| LLaMA-2-70B | 68% | 55% | 45% | -0.9 |
| LLaMA-2-13B | 64% | 43% | 57% | -1.4 |

### Cross-Lingual Analysis

| Language | Average Bias Consistency Rate | Persuasion Rate | Explanation |
|------|---------------|---------|------|
| English | 75% | 42% | Benchmark language, biases are relatively stable |
| French | 70% | 48% | Biases are more easily swayed on social topics |
| Multilingual Average | 72% | 45% | Significant differences across languages |

### Key Findings
- The bias of all tested models is not set in stone—the Judge changes its initial stance in 38% to 57% of cases, indicating that model bias is malleable.
- Smaller models (such as LLaMA-2-13B) are more easily persuaded to change their biases, potentially due to weaker intrinsic consistency mechanisms.
- GPT-4's bias is the most stable, yet it still has a 38% variability rate, showing that even the strongest model risks being swayed by its own adversarial arguments.
- Cross-lingual differences are significant, suggesting that the intensity and robustness of bias are correlated with the language distribution of the training data.

## Highlights & Insights
- The "self-debate" paradigm is highly ingenious—letting the model act as its own adversary bypasses the issue of "mismatched capabilities of external attackers." This test paradigm can serve as a standard tool for bias auditing.
- It reveals the dual risks of bias: both the risk of propagating bias (initial biases influencing the Judge's decision) and the risk of manipulation (biases being easily altered by adversarial arguments).
- Cross-lingual experiments reveal that bias is not language-independent, suggesting that language-specific bias patterns need to be considered in multilingual deployments.

## Limitations & Future Work
- The experiments rely on the models' capability to switch between different roles, which in itself may introduce additional confounding variables.
- Whether the "neutral" referee model is truly neutral remains questionable—even without explicit bias instructions, the model may still possess default inclinations.
- The settings of debate round numbers may affect the conclusions—whether more rounds of debate would further alter the quantitative results of bias robustness is worth exploring.
- The quality of the debate is limited by the prompt design, and different prompts may lead to different conclusions.
- Future work can explore the temporal dynamics of bias—how bias progressively accumulates or dissipates over multiple rounds of conversation.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs Political Compass Test**: PCT is a static single-round test, whereas the self-debate in this paper provides bias evaluation in dynamic interactive scenarios.
- **vs Red teaming**: Traditional red teaming uses human or external model attacks, whereas this paper uses the model itself to attack itself, providing a more unique evaluation perspective.
- **vs Debate-based alignment**: This paper does not use debate to improve the model, but rather uses debate to audit bias.
