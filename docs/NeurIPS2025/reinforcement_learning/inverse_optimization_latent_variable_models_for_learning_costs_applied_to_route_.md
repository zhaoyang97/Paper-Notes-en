# Inverse Optimization Latent Variable Models for Learning Costs Applied to Route Problems

**Conference**: NeurIPS 2025
**arXiv**: [2509.15999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15999)
**Code**: None
**Area**: Reinforcement Learning
**Keywords**: inverse optimization, latent variable model, Fenchel-Young loss, route planning, TSP, VAE

## TL;DR
This paper proposes IO-LVM (Inverse Optimization Latent Variable Model), which employs a VAE-style encoder to map observed COP solutions into a latent cost space. A Fenchel-Young loss combined with black-box solvers (Dijkstra/TSP solver) ensures feasibility at the decoding stage. The model learns the distribution of cost functions from route data without agent labels, and successfully separates navigation preferences of different agents in an unsupervised manner.

## Background & Motivation
**Background**: Inverse optimization (IO) infers objective/cost functions from observed optimal solutions, and is widely applied in route planning, IRL, and related areas. Standard approaches assume a single cost function or rely on contextual conditioning.

**Limitations of Prior Work**:
   - **Single-cost assumption**: In practice, different agents (e.g., different taxi drivers or vessels) have distinct implicit cost preferences, yet standard IO/IRL methods require agent labels to distinguish them.
   - **VAE infeasibility**: For structured outputs such as graph paths, samples decoded by a VAE are unlikely to satisfy constraints (e.g., connectivity, reaching specified endpoints).
   - **Gradient issues with discrete solutions**: The discrete solutions of COPs have near-zero gradients almost everywhere with respect to parameters, impeding end-to-end learning.

**Key Challenge**: A generative model is needed that simultaneously learns the distribution of cost functions across different agents and guarantees that generated solutions satisfy COP constraints, while enabling gradient backpropagation through non-differentiable solvers.

**Core Idea**: A VAE encoder maps observations into a low-dimensional latent **cost space** (rather than solution space); the decoder consists of a parameterized cost mapping followed by a black-box COP solver. Perturbed gradient estimation via the Fenchel-Young loss circumvents the non-differentiability of the solver.

## Method

### Overall Architecture
Observed route $\mathbf{x}_i$ (+ problem instance $\mathbf{p}_i$, e.g., start/end points) → encoder $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$ → latent space $\mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ → decoder $g_\theta: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{Y}$ (mapping to cost space) → black-box solver $\omega(\mathbf{y}^\theta + \epsilon, \mathbf{p})$ (Dijkstra/TSP solver) → reconstructed route $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$

### Key Designs

1. **Cost Space as Latent Representation**

    - **Function**: The VAE latent space encodes not the geometric shape of routes, but the cost function that generates them.
    - **Mechanism**: Different routes from the same agent (with different start/end points) are generated by similar cost functions and thus cluster together in the latent space. A standard VAE clusters by route geometry and cannot disentangle cost factors.
    - **Design Motivation**: Cost functions constitute a more fundamental and compact representation — the geometric space is high-dimensional and endpoint-dependent, whereas the cost space can be very low-dimensional (e.g., 2D suffices to separate three agent types).

2. **Fenchel-Young Loss + Perturbed Gradient Estimation**

    - **Function**: Bypasses the non-differentiable solver to obtain gradients with respect to cost parameters $\theta$.
    - **Mechanism**: $l_{FY}^\epsilon(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{x}) = \langle \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{x} \rangle - \mathbb{E}_\epsilon[\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \langle \mathbf{y} + \epsilon, \mathbf{x} \rangle]$. The gradient is $\nabla_\mathbf{y} l_{FY} = \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\epsilon$ — the difference between the observed route and the solver output under perturbed costs — which is then propagated to $\theta$ via the chain rule.
    - **SGD trick**: By exploiting the linearity of expectation, only a single solver call per sample is required (rather than multiple Monte Carlo evaluations); unbiased gradient estimates are obtained through the double expectation $\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi} \mathbb{E}_\epsilon$.
    - **Design Motivation**: Computational efficiency is critical — the solver is the training bottleneck, and a single call per sample makes the method scalable.

3. **Constraint-Guaranteed Generation**

    - **Function**: By placing a genuine COP solver at the decoding stage, generated routes are guaranteed to satisfy constraints (e.g., connectivity, visiting all nodes).
    - **Comparison with VAE**: A standard VAE decoder is a neural network that cannot guarantee valid routes, especially under complex constraints such as TSP.
    - **Design Motivation**: Feasibility guarantees are essential for safety-critical applications such as route planning.

### Loss & Training
- Total loss: $l(\theta, \phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[l_{FY}(\mathbf{y}^\theta, \mathbf{x})] + \beta D_{KL}(q_\phi \| P(\mathbf{z}))$
- $\beta$-VAE-style KL weighting: jointly controls posterior collapse and denoising capability.
- Reparameterization trick + SGD.
- Solvers: Dijkstra (shortest path) / TSP solver (Hamiltonian circuit).

## Key Experimental Results

### Datasets
- **Synthetic Waxman graphs**: 700 nodes, 3 agent types (high-cost south / high-cost north / unbiased), 6,000 routes.
- **Vessel AIS data**: Danish waters, 2,513 nodes, 2,500 vessel trajectories.
- **Taxi trajectories**: San Francisco Cabspotting, 1,125 nodes, 101,344 trajectories.
- **TSPLIB**: Hamiltonian circuits on burma14 and bayg29 instances.

### Main Results

| Task | IO-LVM | VAE Comparison |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Latent space clustering | Clusters by cost/agent (unsupervised separation of 3 agent types) | Clusters by route geometry (cannot separate agents) |
| Route reconstruction (TSP) | Feasible solutions with high matching rate | Frequently produces infeasible solutions |
| Route distribution prediction | Generates reasonable route distributions via KDE sampling | — |
| Generalization to unseen endpoints | Samples from latent space + solver generates new routes | No feasibility guarantee |

### Key Findings
- A 2D latent space suffices to unsupervisedly separate three agent types with distinct cost preferences (synthetic data).
- In vessel data, certain latent space regions correspond to wider vessels — wide vessels avoid the Øresund Strait and instead take the Great Belt (longer but safer), a previously unlabeled physical factor discovered automatically by IO-LVM.
- IO-LVM supports anomaly detection (latent encodings of test routes deviating from the training distribution) and denoising (regularization strength controlled via $\beta$).

## Highlights & Insights
- **"Solver-as-decoder" design paradigm**: An elegant approach that fully delegates constraint satisfaction in the generative model to classical solvers, while the neural network focuses solely on learning the distribution in cost space. This paradigm generalizes to any COP with an efficient solver.
- **Cost space vs. solution space**: Conventional VAEs learn the distribution in solution space; IO-LVM learns in cost space — the latter is more compact and physically interpretable. A single cost function can generate infinitely many routes (across different endpoints), but the converse does not hold.
- **Unsupervised agent separation**: Without knowing "who traveled this route," IO-LVM automatically infers cost preferences from routes and clusters them accordingly.

## Limitations & Future Work
- Relies on efficient solvers — large-scale instances of NP-hard problems such as TSP may become a bottleneck.
- Assumes observed routes are optimal (shortest paths) under the cost function; in practice, human decisions may be suboptimal.
- The gradient estimation in Proposition 1 for the Fenchel-Young loss requires all feasible paths to have equal length, which holds strictly only for TSP (Hamiltonian circuits) and is approximate for shortest-path problems.
- Quantitative comparisons with other inverse optimization / IRL methods (e.g., MaxEntIRL, differentiable Floyd-Warshall) are absent.

## Related Work & Insights
- **vs. Differentiable Floyd-Warshall (Vlastelica 2020)**: Directly differentiates graph algorithms but scales poorly with graph size; IO-LVM uses black-box solvers with Fenchel-Young gradients, offering greater flexibility.
- **vs. Standard inverse optimization**: Assumes a single cost function and requires agent labels; IO-LVM automatically learns the distribution of cost functions.
- **vs. VAE for COPs**: Standard VAEs provide no feasibility guarantees and learn geometric distributions; IO-LVM guarantees feasibility via the solver and learns cost distributions.
